# The Growth of New Leaders: A 6-Session Bible Study Curriculum

_Designed for Individual Self-Study, Personal Reflection, and Facilitator-Led Group Discussion_

---

## Introduction: The Upside-Down Kingdom of Leadership

Leadership in the Kingdom of God stands in stark contrast to the leadership models of the secular world. While society measures success by influence, titles, and the number of people who serve the leader, scripture defines leadership through the lens of calling, obedience, integrity, and self-sacrificial service.

This curriculum is designed to help new and aspiring leaders lay a firm, biblically grounded foundation for their calling. Over the next six sessions, you will walk through the lives of Old and New Testament leaders to examine:

1. **Calling**: Overcoming insecurity and trusting in God’s presence (Moses).
2. **Preparation**: Learning to serve and follow before leading (Joshua).
3. **Character**: Developing integrity in the secret place (David).
4. **Succession**: Stewarding the Gospel and mentoring others (Paul & Timothy).
5. **Resilience**: Staying focused and standing firm in the face of opposition (Nehemiah).
6. **Servanthood**: Mirroring the ultimate model of upside-down greatness (Jesus).

Whether you are studying these pages alone or in a small group, may the Holy Spirit shape your heart to match the heart of the Shepherd-Leader, Jesus Christ.

## Session 1: The Call of a Leader (Moses)

### 1. Scripture Text

**Exodus 3:1–12 (NIV)**

> 3:1 Now Moses was tending the flock of Jethro his father-in-law, the priest of Midian, and he led the flock to the far side of the wilderness and came to Horeb, the mountain of God. 3:2 There the angel of the Lord appeared to him in flames of fire from within a bush. Moses saw that though the bush was on fire it did not burn up. 3:3 So Moses thought, “I will go over and see this strange sight—why the bush does not burn up.” 3:4 When the Lord saw that he had gone over to look, God called to him from within the bush, “Moses! Moses!” And Moses said, “Here I am.” 3:5 “Do not come any closer,” God said. “Take off your sandals, for the place where you are standing is holy ground.” 3:6 Then he said, “I am the God of your father, the God of Abraham, the God of Isaac and the God of Jacob.” At this, Moses hid his face, because he was afraid to look at God.
> 3:7 The Lord said, “I have indeed seen the misery of my people in Egypt. I have heard them crying out because of their slave drivers, and I am concerned about their suffering. 3:8 So I have come down to rescue them from the hand of the Egyptians and to bring them up out of that land into a good and spacious land, a land flowing with milk and honey—the home of the Canaanites, Hittites, Amorites, Perizzites, Hivites and Jebusites. 3:9 And now the cry of the Israelites has reached me, and I have seen the way the Egyptians are oppressing them. 3:10 So now, go. I am sending you to Pharaoh to bring my people the Israelites out of Egypt.”
> 3:11 But Moses said to God, “Who am I that I should go to Pharaoh and bring the Israelites out of Egypt?” 3:12 And God said, “I will be with you. And this will be the sign to you that it is I who have sent you: When you have brought the people out of Egypt, you will worship God on this mountain.”

**Exodus 4:10–12 (NIV)**

> 4:10 Moses said to the Lord, “Pardon your servant, Lord. I have never been eloquent, neither in the past nor since you have spoken to your servant. I am slow of speech and tongue.” 4:11 The Lord said to him, “Who gave human beings their mouths? Who makes them deaf or mute? Who gives them sight or makes them blind? Is it not I, the Lord? 4:12 Now go; I will help you speak and will teach you what to say.”

### 2. Context & Insight

Moses encounters God while tending the flock in Midian, a rugged desert region east of the Gulf of Aqaba. Historically, Moses was a fugitive living in obscure exile for forty years after attempting and failing to deliver Israel in his own strength (Exodus 2:11–15).

When God calls him from the burning bush, Moses raises five separate objections. His protests—_"Who am I?"_ (Exodus 3:11) and _"I am slow of speech and tongue"_ (Exodus 4:10)—reveal deep insecurity and past trauma. Yet God does not flatter Moses or try to build his self-esteem. Instead, God counters with His covenant promise (_"I will be with you"_) and His supreme sovereignty (_"Who gave human beings their mouths?"_). The focus shifts entirely from the messenger's weakness to God's infinite sufficiency.

- **Key Word Study: "Here I am" (*Hineni*)** (Exodus 3:4) — In ancient Hebrew culture, *Hineni* was far more than an indication of physical location; it was the formal response of a devoted servant signaling total readiness, attentiveness, and surrender to their master. When Moses answered *"Here I am"* (*Hineni*), he surrendered his anonymity in the desert to be sent on God's mission. True leadership begins not when we feel qualified, but when we make ourselves completely available to God.

### 3. Overcoming Insecurity

We are often held back by a silent fear of inadequacy—the feeling that we lack the eloquence, status, or holiness to lead. When faced with responsibility, we list our deficiencies just as Moses did.

But God does not call the qualified; He qualifies the called. In the Kingdom, our weaknesses are not disqualifications; they are the exact arenas where God’s presence is made visible. If you are waiting to feel "ready" before you step up, you are waiting in vain. Leadership begins not when we become strong, but when we surrender our excuses and trust that God's presence is more than enough for the task.

### 4. Personal Prayer

_Father, I confess that I often feel unqualified for the responsibilities You have placed before me. I focus on my weaknesses, my past failures, and my lack of eloquence. Remind me today that my calling does not rest on my ability, but on Your presence. I yield my excuses to You. Help me to say "Hineni"—Here I am—with an open heart. When fear tells me I am not enough, let Your Spirit whisper: "I will be with you." In Jesus' name, Amen._

### 5. Facilitator Discussion Guide

1. What is the specific "Who am I?" insecurity that you struggle with most as a leader?
2. How does God's response to Moses (_"I will be with you"_) change the way we view our limitations?
3. Why is self-reliance actually a barrier to biblical leadership?
4. Recall a time when God used a limitation of yours to accomplish His work.
5. What excuse do you need to surrender to God today in order to move forward in your calling?

## Session 2: The Heart of a Servant (Joshua)

### 1. Scripture Text

**Joshua 1:1–9 (NIV)**

> 1:1 After the death of Moses the servant of the Lord, the Lord said to Joshua son of Nun, Moses’ aide: 1:2 “Moses my servant is dead. Now then, you and all these people, get ready to cross the Jordan River into the land I am about to give to them—to the Israelites. 1:3 I will give you every place where you set your foot, as I promised Moses. 1:4 Your territory will extend from the desert to Lebanon, and from the great river, the Euphrates—all the Hittite country—to the Mediterranean Sea in the west. 1:5 No one will be able to stand against you all the days of your life. As I was with Moses, so I will be with you; I will never leave you nor forsake you.
> 1:6 “Be strong and courageous, because you will lead these people to inherit the land I swore to their ancestors to give them. 1:7 “Be strong and very courageous. Be careful to obey all the law my servant Moses gave you; do not turn from it to the right or to the left, that you may be successful wherever you go. 1:8 Keep this Book of the Law always on your lips; meditate on it day and night, so that you may be careful to do everything written in it. Then you will be prosperous and successful. 1:9 Have I not commanded you? Be strong and courageous. Do not be afraid; do not be discouraged, for the Lord your God will be with you wherever you go.”

### 2. Context & Insight

Joshua assumes leadership at the transition between the Late Bronze and Early Iron Ages. He faces a staggering assignment: stepping into the shoes of Moses, leading a massive nation across the flooded Jordan River, and confronting the heavily fortified city-states of Canaan.

Joshua’s authority was forged through four decades of humble, quiet preparation. Scripture introduces him as Moses' _"aide"_ (*Mesharet* — one who ministers or serves faithfully in the background). God commands Joshua repeatedly to _"be strong and courageous,"_ anchoring this boldness not in military prowess or self-confidence, but in keeping the Book of the Law on his lips and meditating on it day and night.

- **Key Word Study: "Be strong and courageous" (*Chazaq* and *Amats*)** (Joshua 1:6, 7, 9) — In Hebrew, *Chazaq* means to bind firmly, grasp tightly, and remain steadfast under pressure, while *Amats* means to be stout-hearted, alert, and resilient against fear. Together, they describe a courage that is rooted in God's presence rather than human bravado. For emerging leaders, spiritual courage is the resolve to hold fast to God's instructions when circumstances feel overwhelming.

### 3. Under the Shadow of Greatness

Before we are called to lead, we are called to serve. Joshua spent decades carrying out tasks, standing guard, and serving under Moses’ shadow. This was not wasted time; it was the crucible where his character was tested.

If we cannot submit to another leader's authority, we are not fit to hold authority ourselves. True strength is developed when we learn to serve without requiring immediate recognition. When God eventually calls us to step forward, our confidence does not come from our strategic brilliance, but from a habit of quiet obedience and the certainty of His presence.

### 4. Personal Prayer

_Lord, grant me the heart of a servant today. Help me to serve faithfully in the background, without demanding credit, spotlight, or recognition. Teach me to follow well so that I may one day lead well. Strengthen my resolve and give me courage when transitions come. Let Your Word be my guide, and may I meditate on it day and night. Keep me from turning to the right or to the left, and let my security rest in Your presence. Amen._

### 5. Facilitator Discussion Guide

1. Why is serving quietly under another leader so difficult for modern, ambitious people?
2. How did Joshua’s long years of quiet preparation shape his ability to lead Israel?
3. What does it look like practically for a modern leader to keep the Book of the Law on their lips and "meditate on it day and night"?
4. Share an experience where you had to wait or serve in the background. What did God teach you?
5. Why is absolute obedience to God's instructions more critical to leadership success than strategic planning?

## Session 3: Character & Integrity (David)

### 1. Scripture Text

**1 Samuel 16:1–13 (NIV)**

> 16:1 The Lord said to Samuel, “How long will you mourn for Saul, since I have rejected him as king over Israel? Fill your horn with oil and be on your way; I am sending you to Jesse of Bethlehem. I have chosen one of his sons to be king.” 16:2 But Samuel said, “How can I go? If Saul hears about it, he will kill me.” The Lord said, “Take a heifer with you and say, ‘I have come to sacrifice to the Lord.’ 16:3 Invite Jesse to the sacrifice, and I will show you what to do. You are to anoint for me the one I indicate.”
> 16:4 Samuel did what the Lord said. When he arrived at Bethlehem, the elders of the town trembled when they met him. They asked, “Do you come in peace?” 16:5 Samuel replied, “Yes, in peace; I have come to sacrifice to the Lord. Consecrate yourselves and come to the sacrifice with me.” Then he consecrated Jesse and his sons and invited them to the sacrifice.
> 16:6 When they arrived, Samuel saw Eliab and thought, “Surely the Lord’s anointed stands here before the Lord.” 16:7 But the Lord said to Samuel, “Do not consider his appearance or his height, for I have rejected him. The Lord does not look at the things people look at. People look at the outward appearance, but the Lord looks at the heart.” 16:8 Then Jesse called Abinadab and had him pass in front of Samuel. But Samuel said, “The Lord has not chosen this one either.” 16:9 Jesse then had Shammah pass by, but Samuel said, “Nor has the Lord chosen this one.” 16:10 Jesse had seven of his sons pass before Samuel, but Samuel said to him, “The Lord has not chosen these.”
> 16:11 So he asked Jesse, “Are these all the sons you have?” “There is still the youngest,” Jesse answered. “He is tending the sheep.” Samuel said, “Send for him; we will not sit down until he arrives.” 16:12 So he sent for him and had him brought in. He was glowing with health and had a fine appearance and handsome features. Then the Lord said, “Rise and anoint him; this is the one.” 16:13 So Samuel took the horn of oil and anointed him in the presence of his brothers, and from that day on the Spirit of the Lord came powerfully upon David. Samuel then went to Ramah.

**Psalm 78:70–72 (NIV)**

> 78:70 He chose David his servant and took him from the sheep pens; 78:71 from tending the sheep he brought him to be the shepherd of his people Jacob, of Israel his inheritance. 78:72 And David shepherded them with integrity of heart; with skillful hands he led them.

### 2. Context & Insight

This transition occurs during Saul's reign (approx. 1010 BCE). Saul had been rejected by God because of his pride and disobedience, leaving the nation in a vulnerable spiritual and military condition.

When Samuel evaluates Jesse’s sons, he is drawn to Eliab, the oldest, who stands tall and impressive, fitting the conventional Ancient Near Eastern profile of a warrior king. However, the Lord cautions him: _"People look at the outward appearance, but the Lord looks at the heart"_ (1 Sam 16:7). David, the youngest, was out tending the sheep, completely overlooked by his own family.

- **Key Word Study: "Integrity of heart" (*Tom*)** (Psalm 78:72) — From the Hebrew root meaning completeness, wholeness, and blamelessness, *Tom* denotes an undivided heart that is consistent in private and public life. David learned this hidden integrity while defending his father's sheep in isolation from predators. In biblical leadership, integrity of heart (*tom*) must always precede skillful hands (*tebunah*), because what a leader does in secret forms the true foundation of their public ministry.

### 3. Shepherding in the Secret Place

In a culture obsessed with visibility, platform, and public branding, we are continually tempted to prioritize our skills over our character. We work hard to look like leaders on the outside, neglecting the state of our hearts.

But God builds His leaders in the secret place. David’s character was forged in the hills of Bethlehem, tending sheep in isolation. Integrity (*tom*) is what we are when no one else is watching. It is the foundation that keeps our leadership from collapsing under the weight of public exposure. Before you try to lead others with skillful hands, make sure your heart is upright before the Lord.

### 4. Personal Prayer

_Search me, O God, and know my heart today. Forgive me for focusing so much on my public skills while neglecting my private character. Build in me the "tom"—the integrity and wholeness of heart—that stands firm when no one is watching. Teach me the lessons of the sheepfold: faithfulness, humility, and protection. Let my leadership be an overflow of my relationship with You in the secret place. In Jesus' name, Amen._

### 5. Facilitator Discussion Guide

1. How does the world's focus on "outward appearance" tempt us to compromise our character?
2. What does "shepherding in the secret place" look like for you in your current life stage?
3. Why must "integrity of heart" (*tom*) always precede "skillful hands"? What happens when we reverse this order?
4. How can we identify and defeat the "hidden lions and bears" (secret sins, pride, insecurity) in our own lives?
5. What practical steps can we take to build a culture of authentic accountability on our teams?

## Session 4: Mentorship & Succession (Paul & Timothy)

### 1. Scripture Text

**2 Timothy 2:1–7 (NIV)**

> 2:1 You then, my son, be strong in the grace that is in Christ Jesus. 2:2 And the things you have heard me say in the presence of many witnesses entrust to reliable people who will also be qualified to teach others. 2:3 Join with me in suffering, like a good soldier of Christ Jesus. 2:4 No one serving as a soldier gets entangled in civilian affairs, but rather tries to please his commanding officer. 2:5 Similarly, anyone who competes as an athlete does not receive the victor’s crown except by competing according to the rules. 2:6 The hardworking farmer should be the first to receive a share of the crops. 2:7 Reflect on what I am saying, for the Lord will give you insight into all this.

### 2. Context & Insight

Paul writes from a cold Roman dungeon during his second imprisonment under Nero (approx. 66–67 CE). Expecting imminent martyrdom, he writes to Timothy, a young, somewhat timid leader in Ephesus facing heavy doctrinal error and opposition.

Paul focuses on transgenerational succession: Paul ➔ Timothy ➔ Reliable People ➔ Others (2 Tim 2:2). To sustain this chain, Timothy must cultivate three qualities: single-minded focus (the soldier avoiding civilian entanglements), discipline and integrity (the athlete competing according to the rules), and patient labor (the hardworking farmer).

- **Key Word Study: "Entrust" (*Paratithemi*)** (2 Timothy 2:2) — In the ancient Greco-Roman world, *paratithemi* meant placing a sacred, high-value deposit (*paratheke*) into the custody of a trustworthy guardian, who was under solemn obligation to protect and return it unaltered. Paul reminds Timothy that the Gospel is not personal property to modify, but a sacred trust to guard carefully and pass down intact to reliable, qualified leaders.

### 3. Stewarding the Sacred Trust

No leader is permanent. The ultimate test of your leadership is not what you build, but whom you leave behind. Paul, facing execution, was not focused on his own legacy, but on ensuring that Timothy was equipped to pass the Gospel to the next generation.

Mentorship is not a program; it is a costly relationship. It requires us to share in suffering, to cut away civilian entanglements, and to labor patiently like a farmer. We do not own the ministry or the truth we steward; we hold it in trust. Our goal must be to pass the baton cleanly, investing in reliable men and women who will carry the Gospel forward long after we are gone.

### 4. Personal Prayer

_Lord Jesus, thank You for the mentors You have placed in my life, who entrusted the truth to me. Help me to be a faithful link in this chain. Give me the wisdom to identify and invest in the next generation of leaders. Let me be focused like a soldier, disciplined like an athlete, and hardworking like a farmer. Guard me from selfishness, and let my legacy be measured by the faith and growth of those I serve. Amen._

### 5. Facilitator Discussion Guide

1. Who has acted as a "Paul" in your life, and how did their investment shape you?
2. What are the "civilian affairs" that currently threaten to entangle or distract you from your calling?
3. Why is the metaphor of the "hardworking farmer" so relevant to the slow, patient work of mentoring others?
4. How do we balance the immediate demands of getting work done with the slower task of developing new leaders?
5. Name one person you can intentionally invest in or mentor over the next few months. What is your first step?

## Session 5: Courage Under Opposition (Nehemiah)

### 1. Scripture Text

**Nehemiah 4:1–9 (NIV)**

> 4:1 When Sanballat heard that we were rebuilding the wall, he became angry and was greatly incensed. He ridiculed the Jews, 4:2 and in the presence of his associates and the army of Samaria, he said, “What are those feeble Jews doing? Will they restore their wall? Will they offer sacrifices? Will they finish in a day? Can they bring the stones back to life from those heaps of rubble—burned as they are?” 4:3 Tobiah the Ammonite, who was at his side, said, “What they are building—even if a fox climbed up on it, he would break down their wall of stones!”
> 4:4 Hear us, our God, for we are despised. Turn their insults back on their own heads. Give them over as plunder in a land of captivity. 4:5 Do not cover up their guilt or blot out their sins from your sight, for they have thrown insults in the face of the builders.
> 4:6 So we rebuilt the wall till all of it reached half its height, for the people worked with all their heart. 4:7 But when Sanballat, Tobiah, the Arabs, the Ammonites and the people of Ashdod heard that the repairs to Jerusalem’s walls had gone ahead and that the gaps were being closed, they were very angry. 4:8 They all plotted together to come and fight against Jerusalem and stir up trouble against it. 4:9 But we prayed to our God and posted a guard day and night to meet this threat.

**Nehemiah 6:1–4 (NIV)**

> 6:1 When word came to Sanballat, Tobiah, Geshem the Arab and the rest of our enemies that I had rebuilt the wall and not a gap was left in it—though up to that time I had not set the doors in the gates— 6:2 Sanballat and Geshem sent me this message: “Come, let us meet together in one of the villages on the plain of Ono.” But they were scheming to harm me; 6:3 so I sent messengers to them with this reply: “I am carrying on a great project and cannot go down. Why should the work stop while I leave it and go down to you?” 6:4 Four times they sent me the same message, and each time I gave them the same answer.

### 2. Context & Insight

During the Persian Period (approx. 445 BCE), Nehemiah returns to Jerusalem as governor to rebuild the city's protective walls. Broken walls meant vulnerability, economic ruin, and historical shame.

Nehemiah faces fierce opposition from Sanballat (governor of Samaria) and Tobiah (governor of Ammon). They use psychological mockery, threat of physical violence, and slanderous letters accusing him of treason (Nehemiah 6:6) to halt the project. Nehemiah responds with a dual strategy: prayer and vigilance (_"we prayed to our God and posted a guard,"_ 4:9). He refuses to let personal attacks distract him from his mission, maintaining absolute focus.

- **Key Word Study: "Posted a guard" (*Mishmar*)** (Nehemiah 4:9) — In Hebrew, *mishmar* denotes an active, watchful post stationed for protection against sudden attack. Nehemiah demonstrated that faith and practical diligence go hand in hand: earnest prayer to God (*"we prayed"*) was instantly coupled with concrete protective measures (*"posted a guard"*). Biblical leaders do not use faith as an excuse for passivity; they combine continual spiritual dependence with vigilant, practical boundaries.

### 3. Rebuilding in the Midst of Battle

The moment you begin to build something of spiritual value, you will face opposition. It may come as criticism from others, financial pressure, or internal doubt. The enemy’s goal is to make you drop your tools and walk away.

Nehemiah shows us how to respond. He does not engage in petty arguments or try to please his critics. Instead, he takes the attacks to God in prayer and immediately sets guards. He continues to build, refusing to leave the wall.

When distractions call you down to the "plain of Ono," your response must be: _"I am carrying on a great project and cannot go down."_ Stay on the wall, hold your tools, trust God, and keep building.

### 4. Personal Prayer

_Father, I face criticism, doubt, and opposition in my leadership, and it is easy to become discouraged or distracted. Give me the focus of Nehemiah. Help me to say to distractions: "I am carrying on a great project and cannot go down." Give me the wisdom to balance prayer with practical guardrails. Strengthen my hands for the work, and let me find my security in You when the enemy rages. Amen._

### 5. Facilitator Discussion Guide

1. What are the "Sanballats and Tobiahs" (mockery, criticism, or doubts) that you face in your leadership?
2. How do we balance spiritual trust (prayer) with practical preparation (posting a guard) in our daily challenges?
3. How can we distinguish between constructive feedback we need to hear and destructive criticism we need to ignore?
4. What does Nehemiah’s phrase _"I am carrying on a great project and cannot go down"_ mean for your life right now?
5. How can we stand together as a team to protect one another from leadership burnout and opposition?

## Session 6: The Ultimate Model (Jesus)

### 1. Scripture Text

**Mark 10:35–45 (NIV)**

> 10:35 Then James and John, the sons of Zebedee, came to him. “Teacher,” they said, “we want you to do for us whatever we ask.” 10:36 “What do you want me to do for you?” he asked. 10:37 They replied, “Let one of us sit at your right and the other at your left in your glory.” 10:38 “You don’t know what you are asking,” Jesus said. “Can you drink the cup I drink or be baptized with the baptism I am baptized with?” 10:39 “We can,” they answered. Jesus said to them, “You will drink the cup I drink and be baptized with the baptism I am baptized with, 10:40 but to sit at my right or left is not for me to grant. These places belong to those for whom they have been prepared.”
> 10:41 When the ten heard about this, they became indignant with James and John. 10:42 Jesus called them together and said, “You know that those who are regarded as rulers of the Gentiles lord it over them, and their high officials exercise authority over them. 10:43 Not so with you. Instead, whoever wants to become great among you must be your servant, 10:44 and whoever wants to be first must be slave of all. 10:45 For even the Son of Man did not come to be served, but to serve, and to give his life as a ransom for many.”

**John 13:1–17 (NIV)**

> 13:1 It was just before the Passover Festival. Jesus knew that the hour had come for him to leave this world and go to the Father. Having loved his own who were in the world, he loved them to the end. 13:2 The evening meal was in progress, and the devil had already prompted Judas, the son of Simon Iscariot, to betray Jesus. 13:3 Jesus knew that the Father had put all things under his power, and that he had come from God and was returning to God; 13:4 so he got up from the meal, took off his outer clothing, and wrapped a towel around his waist. 13:5 After that, he poured water into a basin and began to wash his disciples’ feet, drying them with the towel that was wrapped around him.
> 13:6 He came to Simon Peter, who said to him, “Lord, are you going to wash my feet?” 13:7 Jesus replied, “You do not realize now what I am doing, but later you will understand.” 13:8 “No,” said Peter, “you shall never wash my feet.” Jesus answered, “Unless I wash you, you have no part with me.” 13:9 “Then, Lord,” Simon Peter replied, “not just my feet but my hands and my head as well!” 13:10 Jesus answered, “Those who have had a bath need only to wash their feet; their whole body is clean. And you are clean, though not every one of you.” 13:11 For he knew who was going to betray him, and that was why he said not every one was clean.
> 13:12 When he had finished washing their feet, he put on his clothes and returned to his place. “Do you understand what I have done for you?” he asked them. 13:13 “You call me ‘Teacher’ and ‘Lord,’ and rightly so, for that is what I am. 13:14 Now that I, your Lord and Teacher, have washed your feet, you also should wash one another’s feet. 13:15 I have set you an example that you should do as I have done for you. 13:16 Very truly I tell you, no servant is greater than his master, nor is a messenger greater than the one who sent him. 13:17 Now that you know these things, you will be blessed if you do them.

### 2. Context & Insight

In 1st-century Mediterranean culture, status was heavily stratified. Washing the dusty feet of dinner guests was a menial task reserved strictly for non-Jewish slaves or the lowest-ranking servants. For a teacher or master to perform this task was a shocking and counter-cultural subversion of social hierarchy.

James and John’s request for prominence in Mark 10 reflects nationalistic expectations of a Messianic conqueror who would rule over the Gentiles. Jesus counters by contrasting Kingdom leadership with Gentile authority (_katakyrieuo_ - to lord it over; _katexousiazo_ - to dominate). Jesus redefines greatness by kneeling with a basin and towel, pointing to His impending crucifixion as the ultimate standard of self-giving service.

- **Key Word Study: "Servant / Serve" (*Diakonos* and *Diakoneo*)** (Mark 10:43–45) — In the ancient Mediterranean world, *diakoneo* described the lowly, unglamorous task of waiting on tables or performing domestic service. Jesus completely redefined greatness by establishing self-giving service—rather than status, rank, or dominating authority—as the supreme mark of leadership in His Kingdom. True authority in Christ is demonstrated not by how many people serve us, but by our willingness to stoop down in love to serve others.

### 3. The Way of the Basin and Towel

Human leadership is naturally drawn to the crown: titles, recognition, authority, and control. Even the disciples, having walked with Jesus for years, argued about who would be the greatest.

But Jesus shows us the way of the towel. In the upper room, knowing that the Father had put all things under His power, Jesus stood up, wrapped a towel around His waist, and washed the feet of His followers—including Judas, who would soon betray Him.

If we want to lead like Jesus, we must be willing to step down. We must lay aside our rights, titles, and pride to wash the feet of those we lead. True authority in Christ is not about how many people serve us, but how many people we serve in love. When we pick up the basin and towel, we show the world the true heart of our Savior.

### 4. Personal Prayer

_Lord Jesus, I am so easily swept up in the pursuit of status, recognition, and control. Forgive my pride. Teach me the way of the towel and the basin. Help me to see those under my care through Your eyes of love. Give me the grace to serve others even when it is costly, dirty, or unappreciated. Let my leadership be marked not by demands, but by self-giving love, mirroring Your sacrifice on the cross. Amen._

### 5. Facilitator Discussion Guide

1. Why is the model of "servant leadership" so difficult to live out in our modern families, workplaces, and ministries?
2. What is the significance of Jesus washing Judas’s feet? How does this challenge the way we treat difficult people under our leadership?
3. What are the practical "basins and towels" in your specific leadership role this week?
4. How does the model of the cross reshape our definition of "success" as a leader?
5. How can we cultivate a culture of mutual service and foot-washing on our leadership teams?
