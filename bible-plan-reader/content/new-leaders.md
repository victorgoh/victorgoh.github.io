# The Growth of New Leaders: A 6-Session Bible Study Curriculum

_Designed for Individual Self-Study, Personal Reflection, and Facilitator-Led Group Discussion_

---

## Introduction: The Upside-Down Kingdom of Leadership

Leadership in the Kingdom of God stands in stark contrast to the leadership models of the secular world. While society measures success by influence, titles, and the number of people who serve the leader, scripture defines leadership through the lens of calling, obedience, integrity, and self-sacrificial service.

This curriculum is designed to help new and aspiring leaders lay a firm, biblically grounded foundation for their calling. Over the next six sessions, you will walk through the lives of Old and New Testament leaders to examine:

1. **Calling**: Overcoming insecurity and trusting in God’s presence (Moses).
2. **Preparation**: Learning to serve and follow before leading (Joshua).
3. **Character**: Developing integrity in the secret place (David).
4. **Succession**: Stewarding the Gospel and mentoring others (Paul & Timothy).
5. **Resilience**: Staying focused and standing firm in the face of opposition (Nehemiah).
6. **Servanthood**: Mirroring the ultimate model of upside-down greatness (Jesus).

Whether you are studying these pages alone or in a small group, may the Holy Spirit shape your heart to match the heart of the Shepherd-Leader, Jesus Christ.

## Session 1: The Call of a Leader (Moses)

### 1. Scripture Text

**Exodus 3:1–12 (NET)**

> 3:1 Now Moses was shepherding the flock of his father-in-law Jethro, the priest of Midian, and he led the flock to the far side of the desert and came to the mountain of God, to Horeb. 3:2 The angel of the Lord appeared to him in a flame of fire from within a bush. He looked—and the bush was on fire, but the bush was not consumed. 3:3 So Moses thought, “I will turn aside to see this amazing sight, why the bush does not burn up.” 3:4 When the Lord saw that he had turned aside to look, God called to him from within the bush and said, “Moses, Moses!” And he said, “Here I am.” 3:5 God said, “Do not come near here. Take your sandals off your feet, for the place on which you are standing is holy ground.” 3:6 He said, “I am the God of your father, the God of Abraham, the God of Isaac, and the God of Jacob.” Then Moses hid his face, because he was afraid to look at God.
> 3:7 The Lord said, “I have surely seen the affliction of my people who are in Egypt, and I have heard their cry because of their taskmasters, for I know their sorrows. 3:8 I have come down to deliver them from the hand of the Egyptians and to bring them up from that land to a land that is both good and spacious, to a land flowing with milk and honey, to the region of the Canaanites, Hittites, Amorites, Perizzites, Hivites, and Jebusites. 3:9 And now, look, the cry of the Israelites has come to me, and I have also seen the oppression with which the Egyptians are oppressing them. 3:10 So now, go, and I will send you to Pharaoh so that you may bring my people, the Israelites, out of Egypt.”
> 3:11 Moses said to God, “Who am I that I should go to Pharaoh and that I should bring the Israelites out of Egypt?” 3:12 He said, “Surely I will be with you, and this will be the sign to you that I have sent you: When you bring the people out of Egypt, you will serve God on this mountain.”

**Exodus 4:10–12 (NET)**

> 4:10 Then Moses said to the Lord, “O my Lord, I am not an eloquent man, neither in the past nor since you have spoken to your servant, for I am slow of speech and slow of tongue.” 4:11 The Lord said to him, “Who gave a mouth to humans, or who makes them mute or deaf, or seeing or blind? Is it not I, the Lord? 4:12 So now go, and I will be with your mouth and will teach you what you must say.”

### 2. Scholar Insights & Context

Moses encounters God while shepherding in Midian, a region east of the Gulf of Aqaba. Historically, Moses was a fugitive who had spent forty years in obscure exile after failing to deliver Israel in his own strength (Exodus 2:11–15).

When God calls him, Moses objects five times. His primary questions—_"Who am I?"_ (Exodus 3:11) and _"I am slow of speech and slow of tongue"_ (Exodus 4:10)—reflect deep insecurity and trauma. Yet God does not reassure Moses of his own abilities. Instead, God counters with His covenant presence (_"Surely I will be with you"_) and His sovereignty (_"Who gave a mouth to humans?"_). The focus shifts entirely from the messenger's weakness to the Sender's infinite power.

- **Key Word Study**: **הִנֵּנִי (_Hineni_)** (Exodus 3:4) translates to "Here I am." In the ancient Near East, this was the formal response of a servant indicating absolute availability and submission to their master. It marks the moment Moses relinquishes his anonymity to be sent on mission.

### 3. Devotional Reflection: Overcoming Insecurity

We are often held back by a silent fear of inadequacy—the feeling that we lack the eloquence, status, or holiness to lead. When faced with responsibility, we list our deficiencies just as Moses did.

But God does not call the qualified; He qualifies the called. In the Kingdom, our weaknesses are not disqualifications; they are the exact arenas where God’s presence is made visible. If you are waiting to feel "ready" before you step up, you are waiting in vain. Leadership begins not when we become strong, but when we surrender our excuses and trust that God's presence is more than enough for the task.

### 4. Personal Prayer

_Father, I confess that I often feel unqualified for the responsibilities You have placed before me. I focus on my weaknesses, my past failures, and my lack of eloquence. Remind me today that my calling does not rest on my ability, but on Your presence. I yield my excuses to You. Help me to say "Hineni"—Here I am—with an open heart. When fear tells me I am not enough, let Your Spirit whisper: "I will be with you." In Jesus' name, Amen._

### 5. Facilitator Discussion Guide

1.  What is the specific "Who am I?" insecurity that you struggle with most as a leader?
2.  How does God's response to Moses (_"I will be with you"_) change the way we view our limitations?
3.  Why is self-reliance actually a barrier to biblical leadership?
4.  Recall a time when God used a limitation of yours to accomplish His work.
5.  What excuse do you need to surrender to God today in order to move forward in your calling?

## Session 2: The Heart of a Servant (Joshua)

### 1. Scripture Text

**Joshua 1:1–9 (NET)**

> 1:1 After the death of Moses the Lord’s servant, the Lord spoke to Joshua son of Nun, Moses’ assistant: 1:2 “Moses my servant is dead. Now get up and cross the Jordan River—you and all these people—to the land I am giving to the Israelites. 1:3 I am giving you every place the sole of your foot treads, just as I promised Moses. 1:4 Your territory will extend from the desert in the south to Lebanon in the north, and from the great river, the Euphrates, in the east (including all of the Hittite territory) to the Great Sea in the west. 1:5 No one will be able to stand against you all the days of your life. As I was with Moses, so I will be with you; I will not abandon you or leave you.
> 1:6 “Be strong and courageous, for you will enable these people to inherit the land I swore to their ancestors I would give them. 1:7 Indeed, be very strong and courageous! Be careful to act in accordance with the entire law my servant Moses commanded you. Do not deviate from it to the right or the left, so that you may have success wherever you go. 1:8 This law scroll must not depart from your mouth! You must memorize it day and night so you can carefully obey all that is written in it, for then you will prosper and be successful. 1:9 I repeat, be strong and courageous! Do not be afraid or discouraged, for the Lord your God is with you wherever you go.”

### 2. Scholar Insights & Context

Joshua takes command at the boundary of the Late Bronze and Early Iron Ages. He faces an incredibly daunting task: stepping into the shoes of Moses, crossing the flooded Jordan River, and leading Israel against the heavily fortified city-states of Canaan.

Joshua’s authority is established on his history of submission. For forty years, he was Moses’ assistant (_mesharet_), serving quietly in the background without seeking the spotlight (Exodus 33:11). God commands him to be _"strong and courageous"_ (_chazaq ve-amats_), linking this grit not to military training, but to unwavering meditation on and obedience to the Law scroll.

- **Key Word Study**: **אָמַץ (_Amats_)** and **חָזַק (_Chazaq_)** (Joshua 1:6, 7, 9) describe a deep internal resolve. _Chazaq_ refers to binding fast, becoming firm and steady, while _amats_ means to be alert, stout, and resilient. Joshua’s strength is to be structurally steady and spiritually alert.

### 3. Devotional Reflection: Under the Shadow of Greatness

Before we are called to lead, we are called to serve. Joshua spent decades carrying out tasks, standing guard, and serving under Moses’ shadow. This was not wasted time; it was the crucible where his character was tested.

If we cannot submit to another leader's authority, we are not fit to hold authority ourselves. True strength is developed when we learn to serve without requiring immediate recognition. When God eventually calls us to step forward, our confidence does not come from our strategic brilliance, but from a habit of quiet obedience and the certainty of His presence.

### 4. Personal Prayer

_Lord, grant me the heart of a servant today. Help me to serve faithfully in the background, without demanding credit, spotlight, or recognition. Teach me to follow well so that I may one day lead well. Strengthen my resolve and give me courage when transitions come. Let Your Word be my guide, and may I meditate on it day and night. Keep me from turning to the right or to the left, and let my security rest in Your presence. Amen._

### 5. Facilitator Discussion Guide

1.  Why is serving quietly under another leader so difficult for modern, ambitious people?
2.  How did Joshua’s long years of quiet preparation shape his ability to lead Israel?
3.  What does it look like practically for a modern leader to "meditate on the Word of God day and night"?
4.  Share an experience where you had to wait or serve in the background. What did God teach you?
5.  Why is absolute obedience to God's instructions more critical to leadership success than strategic planning?

## Session 3: Character & Integrity (David)

### 1. Scripture Text

**1 Samuel 16:1–13 (NET)**

> 16:1 The Lord said to Samuel, “How long do you intend to mourn for Saul? I have rejected him from ruling over Israel. Fill your horn with olive oil and go! I am sending you to Jesse of Bethlehem, for I have selected a king from among his sons.” 16:2 Samuel replied, “How can I go? Saul will hear about it and kill me!” But the Lord said, “Take a heifer with you and say, ‘I have come to sacrifice to the Lord.’ 16:3 Invite Jesse to the sacrifice, and I will show you what you are to do. You must anoint for me the one I point out to you.”
> 16:4 Samuel did what the Lord said. When he arrived in Bethlehem, the elders of the town trembled when they met him. They said, “Do you come in peace?” 16:5 He replied, “Yes, in peace. I have come to sacrifice to the Lord. Consecrate yourselves and come with me to the sacrifice.” He consecrated Jesse and his sons and invited them to the sacrifice.
> 16:6 When they arrived, he saw Eliab and thought, “Surely the Lord’s anointed stands here before him!” 16:7 But the Lord said to Samuel, “Do not look on his appearance or on his physical stature, because I have refused him. For the Lord does not see as man sees; for man looks on the outward appearance, but the Lord looks on the heart.” 16:8 Then Jesse called Abinadab and made him pass before Samuel. But he said, “The Lord has not chosen this one either.” 16:9 Then Jesse made Shammah pass by. But he said, “The Lord has not chosen this one either.” 16:10 Jesse made seven of his sons pass before Samuel. But Samuel said to Jesse, “The Lord has not chosen any of these.”
> 16:11 Samuel said to Jesse, “Are these all the young men?” He replied, “There is still the youngest, but he is shepherding the flock.” Samuel said to Jesse, “Send and bring him, for we will not sit down until he arrives.” 16:12 So he sent and brought him. Now he was ruddy, with beautiful eyes and a handsome appearance. The Lord said, “Get up and anoint him, for this is the one!” 16:13 So Samuel took the horn of oil and anointed him in the presence of his brothers. The Spirit of the Lord rushed upon David from that day forward. Then Samuel got up and went to Ramah.

**Psalm 78:70–72 (NET)**

> 78:70 He chose David his servant, and took him from the sheepfolds; 78:71 from tending the nursing ewes he brought him, to shepherd Jacob his people, and Israel his inheritance. 78:72 So he shepherded them according to the integrity of his heart, and guided them by the skillfulness of his hands.

### 2. Scholar Insights & Context

This transition occurs during Saul's reign (approx. 1010 BCE). Saul had been rejected by God because of his pride and disobedience, leaving the nation in a vulnerable position.

When Samuel evaluates Jesse’s sons, he is drawn to Eliab, the oldest, who stands tall and handsome, fitting the conventional Ancient Near Eastern profile of a warrior king. However, Yahweh warns him: _"Man looks on the outward appearance, but the Lord looks on the heart"_ (1 Sam 16:7). David, the youngest, is out shepherding the sheep, completely overlooked by his own family.

- **Key Word Study**: **תֹּם (_Tom_)** (Psalm 78:72) denotes completeness, integrity, or wholeness. It comes from the root _tamam_, meaning to be complete or finished. David's shepherding was marked by an undivided heart—he fought lions and bears in secret, with no audience but God.

### 3. Devotional Reflection: Shepherding in the Secret Place

In a culture obsessed with visibility, platform, and public branding, we are continually tempted to prioritize our skills over our character. We work hard to look like leaders on the outside, neglecting the state of our hearts.

But God builds His leaders in the secret place. David’s character was forged in the hills of Bethlehem, shepherding sheep in isolation. Integrity (_tom_) is what we are when no one else is watching. It is the foundation that keeps our leadership from collapsing under the weight of public exposure. Before you try to guide others with your hands, make sure your heart is upright before the Lord.

### 4. Personal Prayer

_Search me, O God, and know my heart today. Forgive me for focusing so much on my public skills while neglecting my private character. Build in me the "tom"—the integrity and wholeness—that stands firm when no one is watching. Teach me the lessons of the sheepfold: faithfulness, humility, and protection. Let my leadership be an overflow of my relationship with You in the secret place. In Jesus' name, Amen._

### 5. Facilitator Discussion Guide

1.  How does the world's focus on "outward appearance" tempt us to compromise our character?
2.  What does "shepherding in the secret place" look like for you in your current life stage?
3.  Why must integrity of heart (_tom_) always precede skillfulness of hand (_tebunah_)? What happens when we reverse this order?
4.  How can we identify and defeat the "hidden lions and bears" (secret sins, pride, insecurity) in our own lives?
5.  What practical steps can we take to build a culture of authentic accountability on our teams?

## Session 4: Mentorship & Succession (Paul & Timothy)

### 1. Scripture Text

**2 Timothy 2:1–7 (NET)**

> 2:1 You then, my child, be strong in the grace that is in Christ Jesus 2:2 and what you have heard from me in the presence of many witnesses entrust to faithful people who will be competent to teach others as well. 2:3 Take your share of suffering as a good soldier of Christ Jesus. 2:4 No one in military service becomes entangled in matters of everyday life, so that he may please the one who enlisted him. 2:5 Also, if anyone competes as an athlete, he will not be crowned as the winner unless he competes according to the rules. 2:6 It is the hard-working farmer who ought to be the first to receive a share of the crops. 2:7 Think about what I am saying, for the Lord will give you understanding in everything.

### 2. Scholar Insights & Context

Paul writes from a cold Roman dungeon during his second imprisonment under Nero (approx. 66–67 CE). Expecting martyrdom, he writes to Timothy, a young, somewhat timid leader in Ephesus facing heavy doctrinal error and opposition.

Paul focuses on transgenerational succession: Paul $\rightarrow$ Timothy $\rightarrow$ Faithful Men $\rightarrow$ Others (2 Tim 2:2). To sustain this chain, Timothy must cultivate three qualities: focus (the soldier), discipline/integrity (the athlete), and patient, hard work (the farmer).

- **Key Word Study**: **παραθήκη (_Paratheke_)** (implicitly connected to the word _entrust_ in 2:2) refers to a sacred legal deposit. In the ancient Greco-Roman world, a trustee held a deposit in trust and was legally bound to return it completely unaltered. The Gospel is a sacred _paratheke_ that leaders must protect and hand down faithfully.

### 3. Devotional Reflection: Stewarding the Sacred Trust

No leader is permanent. The ultimate test of your leadership is not what you build, but whom you leave behind. Paul, facing execution, was not focused on his own legacy, but on ensuring that Timothy was equipped to pass the Gospel to the next generation.

Mentorship is not a program; it is a costly relationship. It requires us to share in suffering, to cut away civilian distractions, and to labor patiently like a farmer. We do not own the ministry or the truth we steward; we hold it in trust. Our goal must be to pass the baton cleanly, investing in faithful men and women who will carry the Gospel forward long after we are gone.

### 4. Personal Prayer

_Lord Jesus, thank You for the mentors You have placed in my life, who entrusted the truth to me. Help me to be a faithful link in this chain. Give me the wisdom to identify and invest in the next generation of leaders. Let me be focused like a soldier, disciplined like an athlete, and hard-working like a farmer. Guard me from selfishness, and let my legacy be measured by the faith and growth of those I serve. Amen._

### 5. Facilitator Discussion Guide

1.  Who has acted as a "Paul" in your life, and how did their investment shape you?
2.  What are the "civilian affairs" that currently threaten to entangle or distract you from your calling?
3.  Why is the metaphor of the "hard-working farmer" so relevant to the slow, patient work of mentoring others?
4.  How do we balance the immediate demands of getting work done with the slower task of developing new leaders?
5.  Name one person you can intentionally invest in or mentor over the next few months. What is your first step?

## Session 5: Courage Under Opposition (Nehemiah)

### 1. Scripture Text

**Nehemiah 4:1–9 (NET)**

> 4:1 Now when Sanballat heard that we were rebuilding the wall, he became angry and very upset. He mocked the Jews, 4:2 saying in the presence of his colleagues and the army of Samaria, “What are these feeble Jews doing? Will they restore it themselves? Will they offer sacrifices? Will they finish it in a day? Can they bring these burnt stones back to life from the heaps of rubbish?” 4:3 Tobiah the Ammonite, who was beside him, said, “If even a fox climbed up on what they are building, he would knock down their stone wall!”
> 4:4 Hear, O our God, for we are despised! Return their insults on their own heads and let them be ransomed in a land of captivity! 4:5 Do not cover their iniquity, and do not let their sin be wiped out from before you, for they have insulted the builders.
> 4:6 So we rebuilt the wall, and the entire wall was joined together up to half its height, for the people were determined to work. 4:7 But when Sanballat, Tobiah, the Arabs, the Ammonites, and the Ashdodites heard that the rehabilitation of the walls of Jerusalem was going search-and-rescue, and that the breaches had begun to be closed, they were very angry. 4:8 They all conspired together to come and fight against Jerusalem and to cause havoc in it. 4:9 So we prayed to our God and set a guard as a protection against them day and night.

**Nehemiah 6:1–4 (NET)**

> 6:1 When Sanballat, Tobiah, Geshem the Arab, and the rest of our enemies heard that I had rebuilt the wall and no breach remained in it (even though up to that time I had not set up the doors in the gates), 6:2 Sanballat and Geshem sent this message to me: “Come, let us meet together in the villages on the plain of Ono.” But they were intending to do me harm. 6:3 So I sent messengers to them, saying, “I am doing a great work and I cannot come down. Why should the work stop while I leave it and come down to you?” 6:4 They sent this message to me four times in this same way, and I gave them the same reply.

### 2. Scholar Insights & Context

During the Persian Period (approx. 445 BCE), Nehemiah returns to Jerusalem as governor to rebuild the city's protective walls. Broken walls meant vulnerability, economic ruin, and historical shame.

Nehemiah faces fierce opposition from Sanballat (governor of Samaria) and Tobiah (governor of Ammon). They use psychological mocking, threat of physical violence, and slanderous letters accusing him of treason (Nehemiah 6:6) to halt the project. Nehemiah responds with a dual strategy: prayer and vigilance (_"we prayed... and set a guard,"_ 4:9). He refuses to let personal attacks distract him from his mission, maintaining absolute focus.

- **Key Word Study**: **נָשָׂא (_Nasa_)** and **שָׁלַח (_Shalach_)** (implicitly connected to Nehemiah’s defense) represent carrying weapons while working. Nehemiah structured the labor force so that every builder held a trowel in one hand and a sword in the other. True leadership combines spiritual defense (the sword of the Spirit) with active building.

### 3. Devotional Reflection: Rebuilding in the Midst of Battle

The moment you begin to build something of spiritual value, you will face opposition. It may come as criticism from others, financial pressure, or internal doubt. The enemy’s goal is to make you drop your tools and walk away.

Nehemiah shows us how to respond. He does not engage in petty arguments or try to please his critics. Instead, he takes the attacks to God in prayer and immediately sets guards. He continues to build, refusing to leave the wall.

When distractions call you down to the "plains of Ono," your response must be: _"I am doing a great work and I cannot come down."_ Stay on the wall, hold your tools, trust God, and keep building.

### 4. Personal Prayer

_Father, I face criticism, doubt, and opposition in my leadership, and it is easy to become discouraged or distracted. Give me the focus of Nehemiah. Help me to say to distractions: "I am doing a great work and I cannot come down." Give me the wisdom to balance prayer with practical guardrails. Strengthen my hands for the work, and let me find my security in You when the enemy rages. Amen._

### 5. Facilitator Discussion Guide

1.  What are the "Sanballats and Tobiahs" (mockery, criticism, or doubts) that you face in your leadership?
2.  How do we balance spiritual trust (prayer) with practical preparation (setting a guard) in our daily challenges?
3.  How can we distinguish between constructive feedback we need to hear and destructive criticism we need to ignore?
4.  What does Nehemiah’s phrase _"I am doing a great work and I cannot come down"_ mean for your life right now?
5.  How can we stand together as a team to protect one another from leadership burnout and opposition?

## Session 6: The Ultimate Model (Jesus)

### 1. Scripture Text

**Mark 10:35–45 (NET)**

> 10:35 Then James and John, the sons of Zebedee, came to him and said, “Teacher, we want you to do for us whatever we ask.” 10:36 He said to them, “What do you want me to do for you?” 10:37 They said to him, “Permit one of us to sit at your right hand and the other at your left in your glory.” 10:38 But Jesus said to them, “You do not know what you are asking. Are you able to drink the cup I drink or be baptized with the baptism I am baptized with?” 10:39 They said to him, “We are able.” Then Jesus said to them, “You will drink the cup I drink and you will be baptized with the baptism I am baptized with. 10:40 But to sit at my right hand or at my left is not mine to give. It is for those for whom it has been prepared.”
> 10:41 Now when the other ten heard this, they became angry with James and John. 10:42 Jesus called them together and said to them, “You know that those who are recognized as rulers of the Gentiles lord it over them, and those in high positions use their authority over them. 10:43 But it is not this way among you. Instead, whoever wants to be great among you must be your servant, 10:44 and whoever wants to be first among you must be the slave of all. 10:45 For even the Son of Man did not come to be served but to serve, and to give his life as a ransom for many.”

**John 13:1–17 (NET)**

> 13:1 Just before the Passover Feast, Jesus knew that his time had come to depart from this world to the Father. Having loved his own who were in the world, he loved them to the end. 13:2 The evening meal was in progress, and the devil had already put into the heart of Judas Iscariot, Simon’s son, the idea of betraying him. 13:3 Jesus, knowing that the Father had given all things into his hands, and that he had come from God and was going back to God, 13:4 got up from the meal, set aside his outer garments, and taking a towel, tied it around his waist. 13:5 Then he poured water into a basin and began to wash the disciples’ feet, and to dry them with the towel he had wrapped around himself.
> 13:6 Then he came to Simon Peter. Peter said to him, “Lord, are you going to wash my feet?” 13:7 Jesus replied, “You do not understand what I am doing now, but you will understand after these things.” 13:8 Peter said to him, “You will never wash my feet!” Jesus replied, “If I do not wash you, you have no share with me.” 13:9 Simon Peter said to him, “Lord, not only my feet, but also my hands and my head!” 13:10 Jesus replied, “The one who has bathed needs only to have his feet washed, but is completely clean. And you guys are clean, but not all of you.” 13:11 For he knew the one who was going to betray him; for this reason he said, “Not all of you are clean.”
> 13:12 So when he had washed their feet, put on his outer garments, and sat down again, he said to them, “Do you understand what I have done to you? 13:13 You call me ‘Teacher’ and ‘Lord,’ and you are right, for that is what I am. 13:14 If I then, your Lord and Teacher, have washed your feet, you also ought to wash one another’s feet. 13:15 For I have given you an example, that you should do just as I have done to you. 13:16 I tell you the solemn truth, the slave is not greater than his master, nor is the one who is sent greater than the one who sent him. 13:17 If you know these things, you will be blessed if you do them.

### 2. Scholar Insights & Context

In 1st-century Mediterranean culture, status was heavily stratified. Washing the dusty feet of dinner guests was a menial task reserved strictly for non-Jewish slaves or the lowest-ranking servants. For a teacher or master to perform this task was a shocking and counter-cultural subversion of social hierarchy.

James and John’s request for prominence in Mark 10 reflects nationalistic expectations of a Messianic conqueror who would rule over the Gentiles. Jesus counters by contrasting Kingdom leadership with Gentile authority (_katakyrieuo_ - to lord it over; _katexousiazo_ - to dominate). Jesus redefines greatness by kneeling with a basin and towel, pointing to His impending crucifixion as the ultimate standard of self-giving service.

- **Key Word Study**: **διακονέω (_Diakoneo_)** (Mark 10:45) means to serve or minister. Originally describing waiting on tables, Jesus elevates it to represent the core posture of His mission: self-sacrifice for the redemption of others.

### 3. Devotional Reflection: The Way of the Basin and Towel

Human leadership is naturally drawn to the crown: titles, recognition, authority, and control. Even the disciples, having walked with Jesus for years, argued about who would be the greatest.

But Jesus shows us the way of the towel. In the upper room, knowing that the Father had given all authority into His hands, Jesus stood up, wrapped a towel around His waist, and washed the feet of His followers—including Judas, who would soon betray Him.

If we want to lead like Jesus, we must be willing to step down. We must lay aside our rights, titles, and pride to wash the feet of those we lead. True authority in Christ is not about how many people serve us, but how many people we serve in love. When we pick up the basin and towel, we show the world the true heart of our Savior.

### 4. Personal Prayer

_Lord Jesus, I am so easily swept up in the pursuit of status, recognition, and control. Forgive my pride. Teach me the way of the towel and the basin. Help me to see those under my care through Your eyes of love. Give me the grace to serve others even when it is costly, dirty, or unappreciated. Let my leadership be marked not by demands, but by self-giving love, mirroring Your sacrifice on the cross. Amen._

### 5. Facilitator Discussion Guide

1.  Why is the model of "servant leadership" so difficult to live out in our modern families, workplaces, and ministries?
2.  What is the significance of Jesus washing Judas’s feet? How does this challenge the way we treat difficult people under our leadership?
3.  What are the practical "basins and towels" in your specific leadership role this week?
4.  How does the model of the cross reshape our definition of "success" as a leader?
5.  How can we cultivate a culture of mutual service and foot-washing on our leadership teams?
