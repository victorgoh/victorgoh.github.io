# Growing Leaders

## I.1 Welcome to the journey

Leadership often begins before we feel ready. Someone asks us to coordinate a team, care for a person, lead a discussion, take responsibility for a task, or help others move towards a shared purpose. We may feel excited, uncertain, or both.

It is natural to ask, “What do I need to do?” Leadership does involve skills. We learn to communicate, listen, plan, make decisions, handle disagreements, and help people grow. But Christian leadership begins with a deeper question:

> **Who am I becoming as I learn to serve God and people?**

God is interested not only in what happens through a leader, but also in what happens within a leader. Our understanding, character, relationships, choices, and repeated practices all matter. For this reason, Growing Leaders is not primarily about gaining a position or collecting leadership techniques. It is an invitation to notice how God may be forming you and to take faithful steps in response.

You do not need a formal title to begin. Whenever your actions affect others, whenever someone trusts you with responsibility, or whenever you help people move towards something worthwhile, you have an opportunity to lead.

## I.2 What this course is about

Growing Leaders is a six-session mentoring journey for new and emerging leaders. Each session explores one part of leadership formation:

1. **God Uses Your Story** — noticing how your background and experiences have shaped you.
2. **God Forms Your Character** — learning what difficulty reveals and develops within you.
3. **God Builds Your Faithfulness** — growing through ordinary responsibilities and service.
4. **God Develops Your Gifts** — recognising strengths, skills, spiritual gifts, and fruit.
5. **God Shapes How You Lead People** — building healthy relationships and using influence responsibly.
6. **God Deepens Your Life With Him** — integrating leadership with spiritual depth and continued growth.

The six sessions are an introduction, not a complete account of leadership. They provide a starting point and a simple language for continuing the journey with God and with people who can help you grow.

## I.3 Four dimensions of a growing leader

Each session will invite you to grow in four connected dimensions.

### I.3.1 Head: What do I need to understand?

The Head concerns what you know and how you think. You will explore biblical principles, examine assumptions, and develop a clearer understanding of leadership.

Knowledge alone does not make someone a mature leader, but good leadership needs truth. What you believe about God, people, success, authority, service, and yourself will shape how you lead.

### I.3.2 Heart: Who am I becoming?

The Heart concerns your inner life: your love for God and people, character, integrity, motives, desires, fears, and attitudes.

Leadership brings what is within us closer to the surface. Responsibility may reveal generosity and courage, but it may also reveal insecurity, pride, impatience, resentment, or a desire for approval. This course invites honest reflection without shame. God’s grace allows us to face the truth and grow.

### I.3.3 Hands: What do I need to practise?

The Hands concern what you do. Leadership ability grows through serving, trying, receiving feedback, learning from mistakes, and trying again.

Each session will connect its main idea to an action, conversation, or skill that you can practise in your present circumstances. You do not need to wait for a more important role. Faithful practice can begin where you are.

### I.3.4 Habits: What will help this become a way of life?

Habits are the small actions you repeat. They help truth move from your Head into your Heart and Hands.

A single good intention can be forgotten when life becomes busy. A small habit creates a regular opportunity to remember, respond, and grow. Each session therefore includes one leadership habit to try. You are not being graded on perfect performance. The aim is to experiment, notice what happens, and learn.

## I.4 Six essential practices of a growing leader

Across the six sessions, you will practise a simple progression:

1. **Notice and Learn** — notice experience and learn what it may be revealing.
2. **Pause and Bring It Before God** — pause under pressure, then Notice, Name, Receive, and Respond before God.
3. **Record and Follow Through** — handle commitments with reliability, honest communication, and healthy limits.
4. **Serve, Notice, Ask** — develop gifts through service, observable fruit, and specific feedback.
5. **Listen Before Responding** — understand people before giving an answer or making a decision.
6. **Abide and Review** — remain deeply connected to God while reflecting on leadership and choosing a next step.

Together, these practices describe a growing leader:

> **A growing leader notices experience, welcomes examination, follows through faithfully, develops through service, listens to people, and remains deeply connected to God.**

## I.5 How personal preparation works

We know life is full, schedules get crowded, and leadership responsibilities can feel relentless. This personal preparation is **not academic homework or a test to pass**—it is a protected space for your own soul and leadership formation before you meet with your group.

Think of this time as a gift of margin: an unhurried conversation with God.

### I.5.1 The 3 Golden Rules for Busy Leaders

1. **Bullet Points Over Essays**: You do not need to write polished paragraphs. Single words, quick bullet points, mind maps, or raw questions are completely sufficient.
2. **Choose What Resonates**: If time is short, focus on the Scripture and the questions that speak most directly to your current situation. A single honest reflection is worth more than a dozen rushed answers.
3. **You Control Your Sharing**: Everything you write in this guide is private between you and God. You decide what feels appropriate and safe to share with your group.

### I.5.2 Two Simple Ways to Fit It Into Your Week

- **The 30-Minute Coffee Pause**: Set aside one unhurried 25–35 minute block during the week with your Bible, a warm drink, and a notebook.
- **The "Two-Bite" Rhythm (15 mins + 15 mins)**:
  - *Bite 1 (Mid-week, 15 mins)*: Read the opening scenario, main principle, and Scripture passage on your commute or during lunch.
  - *Bite 2 (Day before meeting, 15 mins)*: Jot down your reflections, pray through the *Bring It Before God* prompts, and pick one insight to bring to the group.

### I.5.3 What each session will ask you to explore

1. Begin with a familiar leadership situation.
2. Consider one main leadership principle.
3. Read and explore a passage of Scripture.
4. Reflect through Head, Heart, Hands, and Habits.
5. Bring what you have learned before God in prayer (*Notice, Name, Receive, Respond*).
6. Choose something specific to practise.
7. Come prepared with **one encouraging insight**, **one honest question**, and **one small step you tried during the week**, and share those three items with the group.

### I.5.4 Bring It Before God

The **Bring It Before God** section turns leadership learning into a personal conversation with God. It reminds us that Christian leadership formation is not merely self-improvement. We bring what we have learned and discovered to God, receive what we need from him, and choose a faithful response.

Each session uses four movements:

1. **Notice:** Pay attention to what has become significant during the lesson.
2. **Name:** Speak honestly to God about the gratitude, motive, fear, failure, desire, relationship, or responsibility involved.
3. **Receive:** Welcome the truth, grace, forgiveness, wisdom, courage, or help you need from God.
4. **Respond:** Choose a concrete act of trust, obedience, repair, service, or changed practice.

This keeps reflection from becoming only self-analysis and keeps the leadership habit grounded in spiritual formation rather than personal discipline alone.

> **Learn the truth → examine your life → bring it before God → practise a faithful next step**

You do not have to produce polished answers. Notes, questions, and unfinished thoughts are welcome. The purpose of preparation is to help you arrive ready for a meaningful conversation.

During the one-hour meeting, your facilitator will help the group:

- review what you have tried since the previous meeting;
- discuss what stood out from the reading and Scripture;
- connect the principle with real experiences;
- examine what may be happening within you;
- practise a leadership or relational skill;
- choose a next step and pray for one another.

The facilitator is not expected to have every answer. Their role is to listen, ask helpful questions, share wisdom, and support your growth. You will often learn as much from the experiences and questions of the other participants as from the written material.

## I.6 A way to participate well

This journey will be most useful if you come with four postures.

### I.6.1 Be present

Give attention to the reading, the conversation, and the people in the group. Put aside distractions where possible. Listen to understand rather than preparing your next answer.

### I.6.2 Be honest

Share what is true rather than what seems impressive. You may choose appropriate limits on what you disclose, but try not to hide behind general or spiritual-sounding answers.

### I.6.3 Be teachable

Remain open to Scripture, the Spirit of God, feedback, and perspectives different from your own. Being teachable does not mean accepting every opinion without thought. It means being willing to listen, test, and learn.

### I.6.4 Be willing to practise

Growth requires action. Try the weekly practice even if it feels small or unfamiliar. If it does not go well, bring that experience back to the group. A failed attempt can still teach you something important.

## I.7 Creating a trustworthy group

Personal reflection and mentoring require trust. At the first meeting, agree on how the group will relate to one another.

Suggested commitments include:

- We will listen without interrupting or rushing to give advice.
- We will speak from our own experience rather than make assumptions about others.
- We will encourage honesty and will not pressure anyone to disclose more than is appropriate.
- We will treat personal sharing as confidential, except where someone may be at risk of harm or where safeguarding responsibilities require help from an appropriate person.
- We will give feedback with kindness, clarity, and humility.
- We will make space for every participant.
- We will pray for one another and follow up on our commitments.

This course is not counselling or trauma therapy. Some reflection may bring painful memories or serious concerns to the surface. You may pause, share only what is appropriate, and seek help from a pastor, counsellor, safeguarding lead, or another qualified person when needed.

## I.8 What growth may look like

You may not notice dramatic change after every session. Leadership formation is often gradual. Growth may look like:

- noticing a reaction before acting on it;
- asking a better question;
- keeping a small commitment;
- apologising without making excuses;
- listening when you would normally defend yourself;
- accepting a less visible responsibility;
- asking someone for feedback;
- recognising a strength and using it to serve;
- making time to be with God rather than relying only on activity.

Do not compare your pace with another person’s. Pay attention to the next faithful step God is placing before you.

## I.9 Before the first module

Take a moment to write a few sentences in response to these questions:

1. Where am I currently serving, influencing, or taking responsibility for others?
2. What do I hope will grow in me through these six modules?
3. What is one question about leadership that I am carrying?
4. What would help me participate openly and consistently in this group?

Finish with a simple prayer in your own words. You might ask God for honesty, courage, wisdom, and a greater love for the people you lead.

> **A prayer for the journey**  
> God, thank you for meeting me where I am. Help me see what you want me to notice, receive what you want to teach me, and practise what you place before me. Form my understanding, my character, my service, and my daily habits. Teach me to lead with humility and love. Amen.

## I.10 Facilitator Guide: Leading Meaningful Conversations

If you are facilitating these sessions, your primary role is not to lecture or provide expert answers. You are creating a safe, hospitable, and unhurried space for emerging leaders to reflect, share honestly, and encounter God.

### I.10.1 Key Facilitation Principles

1. **Guide the Conversation, Don't Dominate It**  
   Aim for participants to do 70% of the talking. Ask open-ended questions (*"What was that experience like for you?"* or *"What did that reveal about your heart?"*) rather than rushing to solve dilemmas.

2. **Model Vulnerability with Healthy Boundaries**  
   When sharing your own stories or struggles, be honest and authentic without making yourself the central focus of the session. A leader who admits unfinished growth invites others to do the same.

3. **Honor Time as a Gift (Suggestions, Not Hard Rules)**  
   The minute allocations beside each segment in the 1-hour conversation outlines (e.g., *7 minutes*, *15 minutes*, *10 minutes*) are **practical pacing guides, not rigid laws**:
   - **Why time tags are included:** They help you keep an eye on the clock so the group doesn't spend 40 minutes on the opening check-in and rush through prayer and application.
   - **Flexibility when the Holy Spirit moves:** If a participant opens up with deep vulnerability or an unexpected pastoral moment arises, feel free to adjust. Shorten another segment or let discussion linger where real formation is happening.
   - **Protect the finish:** Always leave at least 8–10 minutes for personal next steps and praying for one another.

4. **Foster Safety and Confidentiality**  
   Reiterate that personal reflections shared in the group remain within the room. Never force anyone to disclose deeper than they are ready to go.

5. **Point Toward Real-World Practice**  
   Every session should end with concrete intentionality. Ensure each participant leaves with one clear, manageable habit or faithful next step to test before the next session.

# Module 1: God Uses Your Story

> **Main idea:** God uses your background and experiences to shape the way you lead, and he can meet you within your story to bring gratitude, healing, growth, and greater freedom.
>
> **Main Scripture:** Genesis 50:15–21
>
> **Leadership habit:** Notice and Learn

## 1.1 Begin with life

Imagine two new team leaders.

The first grew up in a family where disagreements were discussed openly. People sometimes became frustrated, but they usually returned to the conversation and repaired the relationship. When tension develops in her team, she is willing to name it and invite people to talk.

The second grew up in a family where disagreement felt dangerous. Problems were ignored until someone became angry or withdrew. When tension develops in his team, he quickly changes the subject. He tells himself that he is keeping the peace, but unresolved frustration continues to grow.

Neither leader began with a blank page. Both brought learned expectations into leadership: what conflict means, whether people can be trusted, how authority works, and what to do when relationships become difficult.

The same is true for all of us.

Before we held responsibility, we were already being shaped. Family, culture, church, school, friendships, work, successes, disappointments, opportunities, and losses all contributed to the person we are today. Some experiences gave us strengths. Some taught us unhelpful ways to protect ourselves. Many did both.

Leadership often reveals these patterns because responsibility places us in situations where we must trust, decide, communicate, take risks, handle disappointment, and respond to people who are different from us.

Pause and consider:

- When tension arises, do you tend to move towards it, withdraw, give in, take control, or become defensive?
- Where might you have learned that response?
- How does it help you? How might it limit you?

You do not need a complete answer. Simply begin to notice.

## 1.3 Your story is part of your formation

New leaders sometimes believe that growth begins when they receive a leadership role. In reality, your development began much earlier.

Perhaps you learned responsibility by caring for younger family members. Perhaps moving between cultures taught you to observe carefully and adapt. A teacher may have noticed an ability before you did. A team may have given you your first experience of belonging. A failure may have made you more thoughtful, or criticism may have made you afraid to take initiative.

Looking at your story is not about blaming the past for every present difficulty. It is also not about pretending that every experience was good. It is about becoming more truthful and more free.

When we do not examine our story, we may repeat its patterns without realising it. We may lead out of old fear, seek from a team the approval we once lacked, imitate an unhealthy authority figure, or avoid a responsibility because of a past failure. We may also overlook courage, compassion, endurance, creativity, or wisdom that grew through earlier experiences.

A growing leader learns to ask:

> What has shaped me, how is it influencing me now, and what faithful response is possible?

This kind of reflection does not make the story the centre of leadership. God remains at the centre. Reflection helps us bring more of our real selves into relationship with God, where gratitude, repentance, healing, learning, and change can begin.

## 1.4 Explore Scripture: Joseph looks back

Read **Genesis 50:15–21**.

This scene comes near the end of a long and difficult family story. Joseph had been favoured by his father, hated by his brothers, sold into slavery, falsely accused, imprisoned, forgotten, and eventually entrusted with great responsibility in Egypt. Years later, famine brought his brothers to him for help. After their father died, the brothers feared Joseph would finally take revenge.

Joseph did not deny that they had intended harm. He named it honestly. Yet he also recognised that their intention did not have the final word. God had been at work to preserve life through circumstances that included human wrongdoing and deep suffering.

Joseph’s response did not make the brothers’ actions good, excuse their responsibility, or suggest that suffering itself should be celebrated. It revealed that evil and pain did not place his life beyond God’s reach. Joseph could look back without allowing revenge to determine his next action. His reading of the past enabled him to choose reassurance and provision in the present.

Notice four movements in the passage:

1. **The past is remembered.** The brothers know that real harm was done.
2. **Wrong is named as wrong.** Joseph does not rewrite their actions as harmless.
3. **God’s larger work is recognised.** Human intention is not treated as ultimate.
4. **A different future is chosen.** Joseph refuses revenge and acts to preserve life.

Reflect on the passage:

- What does Joseph acknowledge about human responsibility?
- What does he appear to believe about God?
- How does his understanding of the past shape the way he uses power in the present?
- What would have been different if Joseph had used his authority to settle an old score?

## 1.5 Reading our stories with humility

Joseph’s story is extraordinary. We should be careful not to assume that we can always identify exactly why something happened or confidently explain God’s purpose in another person’s pain.

Reading our story with faith is not saying:

- “Everything that happened was good.”
- “The harm did not matter.”
- “I should remain in an unsafe situation.”
- “I know precisely why God allowed this.”
- “If I were spiritually mature, I would no longer feel pain.”

Instead, it may mean saying:

- “This truly affected me.”
- “Some of what happened was good, and some was not.”
- “I can thank God for what I received and grieve what I lost.”
- “I want to notice the strengths and patterns I carry forward.”
- “My past has influenced me, but it does not have to control every future response.”
- “God can meet me here and lead me towards truth, healing, and faithful action.”

Some parts of your story may require more care than this group can provide. Wise leadership includes knowing when to seek appropriate pastoral, counselling, safeguarding, or professional help. You are not required to explore or disclose painful experiences in detail during this session.

As you become more aware of how your story has shaped you, you can begin noticing what God wants to form within you—especially when leadership brings pressure.

## 1.6 Four dimensions of formation

### 1.6.1 Head: Understand your formation

Your leadership did not begin with your current responsibility. You bring a history into every role and relationship. That history may have developed strengths, expectations, sensitivities, and protective responses.

The central leadership principle is:

> **Self-awareness helps a leader respond to the present instead of unconsciously repeating the past.**

Self-awareness is not endless self-analysis. It is the ability to notice what is influencing you so that you can respond with greater truth, love, and freedom.

Write briefly:

1. What is one strength your story has helped develop in you?
2. What is one leadership assumption you may have learned from family, culture, church, education, or work?
3. How might that assumption help you? How might it limit you?

### 1.6.2 Heart: Notice what happens within you

Our strongest reactions sometimes point towards an important part of our story. A leader who fears being ignored may dominate a conversation. Someone who fears disappointing others may agree to every request. Someone who experienced unpredictable authority may resist even healthy direction.

Think of a recent situation in which you had a strong reaction while serving, working with others, or carrying responsibility.

- What happened?
- What did you feel?
- What did you want in that moment?
- What were you afraid might happen?
- Does the reaction feel familiar?
- What might a more truthful and loving response look like?

Be curious before becoming critical. The aim is not to shame yourself but to understand what needs God’s attention and grace.

### 1.6.3 Hands: Create a simple leadership story map

Draw a line representing your life from childhood to the present. Mark **three to five experiences** that have influenced the way you relate, serve, or lead. Choose experiences that are appropriate to explore in this setting.

These might include:

- a family responsibility;
- a move or cultural transition;
- a friendship or significant relationship;
- an early opportunity to serve;
- a leader who encouraged or disappointed you;
- a success or failure;
- a season of hardship or change;
- an experience of God’s guidance or care.

For each experience, note:

| Experience | What did I learn or begin to believe? | What strength or pattern developed? | How might it affect my leadership now? |
|---|---|---|---|
|  |  |  |  |
|  |  |  |  |
|  |  |  |  |

Look at the map as a whole. What theme, repeated pattern, or question do you notice?

Circle one experience you would like to understand more clearly. You may discuss it with your facilitator, a trusted mentor, or another appropriate person. You are not required to share it with the whole group.

### 1.6.4 Habits: Notice and Learn

Leaders learn from experience when they create space to notice it. This week, practise a brief daily review.

**Purpose:** To become more attentive to what your experiences and reactions may be revealing.

**Cue:** Choose a regular point near the end of your day—for example, after dinner, before closing your laptop, or before going to bed.

**Practice:** Take three to five minutes and write:

1. What was one significant moment today?
2. What happened within me?
3. What might this reveal about how my story has shaped me?
4. What might God be inviting me to learn or practise?

**Review:** Before the next session, read your entries and look for a repeated reaction, strength, concern, or invitation to grow.

**Support:** Tell the group when and where you plan to do the review. If helpful, set a reminder or ask another participant to check in once during the week.

Use a simple format:

| What happened? | What happened within me? | What might I learn or practise? |
|---|---|---|
|  |  |  |

If you miss a day, begin again the next day. The purpose is attentive learning, not maintaining a perfect record.

## 1.7 Bring It Before God

Take a few quiet moments with these four movements.

### 1.7.1 Notice

What part of your story, reaction, strength, or pattern has your attention?

### 1.7.2 Name

What can you name honestly before God? This may include gratitude, grief, confusion, regret, fear, or hope.

### 1.7.3 Receive

What truth, grace, wisdom, courage, or help do you need from God?

### 1.7.4 Respond

What is one faithful next step? It may be to give thanks, change a response, seek feedback, repair a relationship, establish a boundary, or ask an appropriate person for help.

Write a brief prayer:

> God, thank you for ________________________________________________.  
> I bring you _______________________________________________________.  
> Help me to see ___________________________________________________.  
> My next faithful step is ___________________________________________.

## 1.8 Prepare to share

Bring your notes to the mentoring conversation. Be ready to share:

1. One idea from the reading or Scripture that stood out to you.
2. One strength, assumption, or pattern you noticed in your story.
3. When and where you will practise Notice and Learn.

Choose what is appropriate to share. You can participate honestly without telling the group every detail of your story.

> 🔒 *Use this private space to write down your thoughts before your group meeting. For those who prefer pen and paper, feel free to write these in your journal.*

* **1. One insight or moment that resonated most with me:**
> Write your response here.

* **2. One honest question or struggle I want to bring to the group:**
> Write your response here.

* **3. My personal next step or prayer focus for this week:**
> Write your response here.

# Facilitator guide

## 1.9 Purpose of the conversation

By the end of the session, participants should:

- understand that their story influences the way they lead;
- identify at least one strength and one assumption or pattern shaped by experience;
- see that honest reflection can create greater freedom in present leadership;
- choose an appropriate next step;
- commit to the habit of Notice and Learn for the coming week.

This session is not intended to produce a complete life history or resolve painful experiences. Its purpose is to introduce attentive, grace-filled reflection.

## 1.10 Prepare as facilitator

Before the meeting:

- Read Genesis 50:15–21 and the participant material.
- Complete a small version of your own leadership story map.
- Choose one appropriate personal example you can share briefly.
- Decide how you will explain confidentiality and its limits.
- Know the relevant pastoral, safeguarding, or professional support pathways in your context.
- Pray for humility, sensitivity, and discernment.

## 1.11 One-hour conversation plan

### 1.11.1 Welcome and establish trust — 7 minutes

Open in prayer and invite each person to answer:

> What is one hope you bring to these six sessions?

Introduce the purpose and briefly agree on group commitments: listening, appropriate confidentiality, no forced disclosure, space for every person, and gracious feedback.

### 1.11.2 Enter through experience — 8 minutes

Ask:

> Think of a leader who influenced you. Without naming the person, what is one leadership behaviour you may have learned from them—either something you want to imitate or something you want to avoid?

Give every participant time to answer. Do not evaluate the examples. Notice how experience can shape both positive commitments and protective reactions.

### 1.11.3 Explore Scripture — 12 minutes

Invite someone to summarise Genesis 50:15–21 in their own words. Use two or three questions:

- What did Joseph name honestly about the past?
- How did his understanding of God affect the way he used power?
- What does this passage show us about remembering harm without being ruled by revenge?
- What should we be careful not to claim when interpreting our own or another person’s suffering?

Keep the focus on the text and its leadership implications. Do not imply that harmful actions become good because God can work in and beyond them.

### 1.11.4 Share and examine — 15 minutes

> **Halfway point:** The group will reach the halfway point of the one-hour conversation near the beginning of this activity.

Invite each person to share one appropriate insight from their story map:

- What is one strength your story helped develop?
- What leadership assumption or repeated response have you noticed?
- How does it help you, and when might it limit you?

Helpful follow-up questions include:

- “Can you give a recent example?”
- “What tends to happen within you in that situation?”
- “What do you think you are trying to protect or secure?”
- “How might another person experience your response?”
- “What different response would express truth and love?”

Do not ask participants to explain painful experiences in detail. If someone becomes distressed, slow down, acknowledge what has surfaced, offer choice about continuing, and follow the appropriate support process after the session.

### 1.11.5 Practise noticing — 8 minutes

Offer this short scenario:

> A team member questions your plan in front of the group. You immediately feel embarrassed and speak sharply. Later, you keep replaying the moment and blaming the team member.

Ask the group to practise the three daily-review questions:

1. What happened?
2. What may have happened within the leader?
3. What could the leader learn or practise next time?

Then ask each participant to apply the same questions privately to a recent low-risk situation of their own. Allow one or two minutes to write before inviting voluntary sharing.

### 1.11.6 Make a commitment — 6 minutes

Ask each participant to complete:

> I will practise Notice and Learn at __________ after/before __________. At the end of the week, I will look for __________.

Check that the cue is specific and realistic. Invite participants to record it or message it to the group.

### 1.11.7 Pray — 4 minutes

Invite each person to name one quality they want God to form in them through their story. Pray briefly for the person beside you, asking for truth, grace, courage, and growth.

## 1.12 Facilitator cautions

- Do not treat every past event as a lesson deliberately sent by God.
- Do not hurry participants towards forgiveness, reconciliation, or disclosure without attention to truth, safety, responsibility, and appropriate support.
- Do not diagnose participants or interpret their story for them.
- Do not allow one participant’s story to consume the entire meeting. Honour it and arrange follow-up if more time is needed.
- Do not let reflection remain abstract. Help each participant identify one present leadership implication and one small practice.

## 1.13 Between sessions

Send one brief, non-intrusive check-in during the week:

> How is Notice and Learn going? No need for a long answer—what are you beginning to notice and learn?

At the beginning of Session 2, allow each person to share:

- how often they attempted the habit;
- one thing they noticed;
- what helped or made the practice difficult;
- whether they want to continue or adjust it.

# Module 2: God Forms Your Character

> **Main idea:** God forms your character as pressure reveals what is within you, his Word brings truth, and you respond with integrity and obedience.
>
> **Main Scripture:** 1 Samuel 24:1–12
>
> **Leadership habit:** Pause and Bring It Before God

## 2.1 Begin with life

A leader has worked hard on a project, but another person receives the recognition. Later, the leader discovers a mistake that the other person made. Mentioning it publicly would make the leader look more capable.

What happens next may be decided in a few seconds. Resentment offers a reason to expose the mistake. Ambition calls it honesty. The leader can easily justify doing something that serves a hidden motive.

Pressure does not always arrive as a crisis. It may come through criticism, delay, fatigue, disappointment, temptation, conflict, success, or an unexpected opportunity. It narrows our attention and strengthens our desire for relief, control, approval, or revenge. In those moments, what is within us becomes more visible.

Think of a recent pressured moment:

- What did you want immediately?
- What response felt easiest?
- What did your reaction reveal?

Pressure does not tell the whole truth about your character, but it often tells you something worth noticing.

Your story helps explain some of the patterns you bring into leadership. Pressure often brings those patterns to the surface, where they can be examined and formed by God.

## 2.3 Character and leadership

Character is the developing pattern of who we are and how we respond. It is expressed in choices made both publicly and privately: telling the truth, keeping a confidence, admitting a mistake, treating people fairly, using authority carefully, and obeying God when another course appears easier.

Leadership skill can help a person gain influence. Character affects what that person does with influence. A gifted leader without integrity can damage people, misuse trust, and hide behind results. A leader of character is not flawless, but is increasingly truthful, responsible, repentant, and aligned between belief and action.

Formation under pressure includes:

- noticing what a situation reveals;
- bringing motives and reactions honestly before God;
- choosing what is right rather than what is merely convenient;
- admitting failure without excuse;
- repairing harm where possible;
- receiving help and accountability;
- learning a different response for the future.

Failure can become formative when it leads to truth and change. Hiding, blaming, and defending usually deepen the problem.

## 2.4 Explore Scripture: David in the cave

Read **1 Samuel 24:1–12**.

David had been anointed as Israel’s future king, but Saul remained king and was trying to kill him. In the cave, David unexpectedly had Saul within reach. His companions interpreted the opportunity as permission from God to act. David secretly cut off a corner of Saul’s robe, but his conscience troubled him. He stopped his men from attacking and later addressed Saul openly from a distance.

The situation contained real injustice. David was not imagining Saul’s hostility, and refusing to kill Saul did not mean pretending Saul was safe. David maintained distance while refusing revenge.

Notice what the pressure exposed:

1. **An opportunity could be interpreted to support desire.** David’s men gave the moment spiritual meaning that favoured immediate action.
2. **A small action affected David’s conscience.** He was willing to reconsider rather than silence that discomfort.
3. **David restrained both himself and his followers.** Character influenced how he exercised leadership over the group.
4. **He spoke truth without taking revenge.** David named the injustice and appealed for judgment while maintaining an appropriate boundary.

Reflect:

- What reasons could David have used to justify killing Saul?
- What helped him resist the pressure of the moment and the advice of his companions?
- How did he combine restraint, truth, and safety?
- What does the passage suggest about an opportunity that seems to offer exactly what we want?

## 2.5 Pressure can distort judgment

Under pressure, we commonly tell ourselves incomplete stories:

- “I have no choice.”
- “They deserve it.”
- “Everyone does this.”
- “The result is good, so the method does not matter.”
- “If I admit this, people will lose respect for me.”
- “This opportunity must be God’s will because the door is open.”

A pause creates room to test the story. We can ask what is true, what love requires, what responsibility belongs to us, and whose wise counsel we need.

Integrity does not mean remaining passive in the face of harm. A faithful response may include a difficult conversation, a firm boundary, reporting misconduct, or seeking help. Restraint is not the same as avoidance. The question is whether our response is governed by truth, love, justice, and appropriate responsibility rather than by an unchecked reaction.

## 2.6 Let God’s Word shape you

Leaders can approach Scripture mainly to prepare a talk, answer someone else’s question, or support a decision already made. Scripture then becomes material we use rather than truth that first examines and forms us.

God’s Word may encourage and reassure us. It may also confront an ambition, expose a fear, correct an attitude, reorder a priority, or call us to an action we would rather avoid. Receiving biblical truth means more than understanding or explaining it. We allow it to address us personally and respond with trust and obedience.

The central question is:

> **Am I allowing God’s Word to shape me before I use it to guide others?**

Use this simple movement:

1. **Receive:** Read the passage attentively and remain open to being addressed by its truth.
2. **Understand:** Consider its context, purpose, and meaning rather than isolating a convenient phrase.
3. **Examine:** Ask what the truth reveals about your beliefs, motives, relationships, ambitions, or behaviour.
4. **Respond:** Identify an act of trust, obedience, confession, repair, waiting, or changed practice.
5. **Confirm:** Seek mature counsel when the interpretation or application carries significant consequences.

Samuel had to learn to listen to God and handle a difficult message responsibly (1 Samuel 3). He did not manufacture the message or use it to establish his importance. He listened, received help in recognising what was happening, and spoke with honesty. In the same way, leaders need both openness and humility.

This does not mean treating every thought, emotion, coincidence, or strong impression as a direct message from God. Personal impressions should be tested through Scripture in context, the character and way of Jesus, mature counsel, Christian community, and responsible attention to consequences. No claimed message from God should be used to manipulate people, avoid accountability, override safeguarding, or justify conduct contrary to Scripture.

# Module 3: God Builds Your Faithfulness

> **Main idea:** God builds faithfulness through the ordinary responsibilities, relationships, limits, and opportunities entrusted to you.
>
> **Main Scripture:** Luke 16:10–13
>
> **Leadership habit:** Record and Follow Through

## 3.1 Begin with life

Two volunteers want greater responsibility.

One is confident, creative, and eager to lead visible projects. Yet messages go unanswered, small tasks are forgotten, and others regularly finish what this person began.

The other serves in less visible ways. This person clarifies expectations, communicates early when a problem arises, completes agreed tasks, and asks for feedback. People increasingly trust this volunteer with more responsibility.

Potential matters, but trust is usually built through patterns. Leadership grows when people learn that our words and actions are dependable.

Character is not formed only in moments of unusual pressure. It is also formed through the ordinary responsibilities we carry every day.

Faithfulness is not glamorous. It may look like arriving prepared, caring for equipment, following up with a person, completing an administrative task, or saying early that we cannot meet a deadline. These small actions affect people. They either strengthen or weaken trust.

## 3.3 Faithfulness is stewardship

Stewardship means caring responsibly for something entrusted to us. A leader may be entrusted with time, information, money, relationships, authority, opportunities, or a specific task. None should be treated casually.

Faithfulness includes:

- understanding what has been entrusted;
- doing what was agreed;
- giving appropriate care to both visible and hidden work;
- communicating when circumstances change;
- asking for help before a preventable crisis;
- learning from feedback;
- respecting personal and relational limits.

Faithfulness is not saying yes to everything. Overcommitment may look generous at first, but repeated failure to deliver burdens other people. A truthful no, a negotiated deadline, or a request for support may be more responsible than an enthusiastic promise that cannot be kept.

Nor is faithfulness the same as perfection. Reliable leaders still make mistakes. The difference is that they acknowledge them, communicate, repair what they can, and improve their process.

## 3.4 Explore Scripture: Trustworthy in little and much

Read **Luke 16:10–13**.

Jesus connects faithfulness in small matters with faithfulness in larger ones. He also speaks about handling what belongs to another and about the impossibility of serving two masters.

The passage challenges the idea that small responsibilities do not matter. How we handle them reveals something about our loyalties and readiness. The issue is not the public size of a task but the trust involved in it.

Notice:

1. **Little and much are connected.** Present patterns do not automatically change when responsibility increases.
2. **Character appears in ordinary stewardship.** Reliability is formed before the larger opportunity arrives.
3. **Some things belong to another.** Leadership often means caring for people and resources that are not ours to use for personal status.
4. **Faithfulness is connected to loyalty.** What we serve shapes the choices we make.

Reflect:

- Which “small” responsibilities are easy for you to dismiss?
- What does it mean to handle another person’s trust carefully?
- How might ambition compete with faithfulness?
- What pattern in your present responsibilities would become harmful if your influence increased?

## 3.5 The hidden work of reliability

Reliable people often use simple practices that others do not see. They record commitments, clarify who is responsible, break work into steps, review progress, and communicate early.

When follow-through is difficult, the answer is not always “try harder.” The cause may be:

- an unclear request;
- too many commitments;
- fear of disappointing people by saying no;
- poor planning;
- avoiding an unpleasant task;
- a need for training or support;
- a health or life circumstance requiring adjustment.

Mature stewardship asks both, “How can I become more dependable?” and “What is a healthy and honest commitment for me to make?”

God often builds trustworthy leaders through repeated acts of ordinary faithfulness long before their work becomes visible.

### 3.6.4 Habits: Record and Follow Through

# Module 4: God Develops Your Gifts

> **Main idea:** God develops your gifts as you serve, practise, notice fruit, receive feedback, and depend on his grace.
>
> **Main Scripture:** 1 Peter 4:7–11
>
> **Leadership habit:** Serve, Notice, Ask

## 4.1 Begin with life

A new leader wants to know their spiritual gift. They complete several questionnaires and receive different results. One says leadership, another encouragement, and another teaching. The labels create more confusion than clarity.

Meanwhile, this person regularly notices newcomers, helps them feel included, and connects them with others. People say they feel seen and welcomed. A gift may already be visible in the fruit of ordinary service, even before the leader knows what to call it.

Many people look for a label before they begin. Often discovery works in the opposite direction: serve, notice, listen, practise, and reflect. A clearer understanding develops over time.

As you serve faithfully in present responsibilities, your strengths and gifts often become clearer.

## 4.3 Gifts are entrusted for service

Leaders bring several kinds of capacity:

- **Natural strengths:** patterns that come relatively easily, such as organising, analysing, creating, or relating.
- **Learned skills:** abilities developed through instruction and practice, such as facilitating, planning, listening, or resolving conflict.
- **Spiritual gifts:** expressions of God’s grace through which believers serve and build up others.
- **Experience:** knowledge and wisdom gained through life, work, culture, suffering, and service.

These categories can overlap. The most important question is not, “What impressive label belongs to me?” but, “What has God entrusted to me, and how can it serve others?”

A strength is not automatically mature. Communication can encourage or manipulate. Confidence can provide direction or silence others. Compassion can offer care or avoid necessary truth. Gifts need character, skill, and community discernment.

## 4.4 Explore Scripture: Stewards of God’s grace

Read **1 Peter 4:7–11**.

Peter addresses believers living under difficulty. He calls them to clear-minded prayer, deep love, hospitality, and service. Each person is to use what they have received as a faithful steward of God’s varied grace. Speaking and serving are done through God’s provision and for God’s glory.

Notice:

1. **Each person has received something to use.** No participant is unnecessary.
2. **Gifts are expressions of grace, not trophies of achievement.** They are received and entrusted.
3. **The purpose is service.** Gifts move outward for the good of others.
4. **God provides what service requires.** Leaders need dependence, not self-sufficiency.
5. **God receives the glory.** Visibility is not the measure of value.

Reflect:

- How does stewardship change the way you think about a gift?
- What dangers arise when a leader uses a gift to establish identity or status?
- Why do gifts need both love and dependence on God?
- What kind of fruit would show that a gift is genuinely serving people?

## 4.5 Four ways gifts become clearer

### 4.5.1 Service

We learn by taking real responsibility. Serving exposes both capacity and areas needing growth.

### 4.5.2 Fruit

Look for what helps people, strengthens the community, advances a worthwhile purpose, and reflects Christlike character. Enjoyment or ease alone is not enough.

### 4.5.3 Feedback

Others may recognise patterns we overlook. Useful feedback includes specific examples rather than flattering labels.

### 4.5.4 Development

Potential becomes more dependable through learning, practice, correction, and repetition. A gift does not remove the need for preparation.

Comparison interferes with this process. A visible gift is not necessarily more valuable, and another person’s strength does not reduce yours. Healthy teams need difference.

God provides the gift, but leaders remain responsible for developing the character and skill needed to use it well.

## 4.6 Four dimensions of formation

### 4.6.1 Head: Understand gifts as stewardship

The central leadership principle is:

> **A gift is entrusted grace to be developed and used for the good of others.**

Write briefly:

1. What distinction between strengths, skills, gifts, and experience is helpful to you?
2. Where have you seen good fruit from something you contributed?
3. What ability might become more useful with development?

### 4.6.2 Heart: Examine identity and comparison

Consider:

- Do you need recognition to feel that your contribution matters?
- Do you minimise a strength because it feels ordinary to you?
- Are you comparing yourself with someone more visible?
- Are you holding back because you fear being evaluated?
- Have you confused giftedness with maturity?

Choose one. What truth about grace, service, and belonging do you need to receive?

### 4.6.3 Hands: Design a development experiment

Choose one possible strength or gift to explore during the next month.

|---|---|

| What preparation or skill do I need? |  |
| What fruit will I look for? |  |
| Who can observe and give feedback? |  |

Keep the experiment small enough to complete. Examples include facilitating part of a meeting, encouraging a specific person, organising one task, explaining a short passage, or helping resolve a practical need.

### 4.6.4 Habits: Serve, Notice, Ask

**Purpose:** To develop a possible gift through real service, observable fruit, specific feedback, and repeated practice.

**Cue:** When you receive a small, appropriate opportunity to practise a possible strength or gift.

**Practice:**

1. **Serve:** Try one small act of service using the possible gift.
2. **Notice:** Observe whom it helped and what fruit, response, or difficulty appeared.
3. **Ask:** Request specific feedback: What was helpful? What effect did it have? What could I develop?
4. **Grow:** Choose one adjustment and practise again.

**Review:** Record the service, fruit, feedback, and next adjustment. Look for repeated patterns over time rather than drawing a conclusion from one experience.

**Support:** Tell your group what service you will try, whom it is intended to help, and whom you will ask for feedback.

Feedback is information, not a verdict on your identity. Receive it thoughtfully, test it, and decide what to practise.

# Module 5: God Shapes How You Lead People

> **Main idea:** God shapes how you lead people through relationships, responsibility, disagreement, feedback, and opportunities to use influence in the way of Jesus.
>
> **Main Scripture:** Mark 10:35–45
>
> **Leadership habit:** Listen Before Responding

## 5.1 Begin with life

A leader presents a plan to the team. One member raises a concern. The leader hears resistance, interrupts, and explains the plan more forcefully. The team member stops speaking, and the meeting appears to move on smoothly.

Later, the concern becomes a real problem. The leader wonders why no one warned them.

Leadership always involves people who have their own knowledge, fears, hopes, limits, and perspectives. A decision can be technically correct and relationally damaging. A kind leader can also avoid necessary clarity. Relational maturity requires both love and truth.

Gifts are not given for private identity or recognition. They are entrusted to us so that we can serve real people in healthy and responsible ways.

## 5.3 Influence is for service

Authority and influence can help a group move towards a good purpose. They can also be used to protect the leader’s image, secure compliance, or silence discomfort.

Healthy leaders:

- listen before deciding they understand;
- clarify expectations and responsibility;
- use authority for the shared purpose and the good of people;
- welcome appropriate disagreement;
- address problems directly and respectfully;
- distinguish correction from humiliation;
- repair relationships when they contribute to harm;
- recognise boundaries and power differences.

Serving people does not mean pleasing everyone. A leader may need to make an unpopular decision, challenge harmful behaviour, or say no. The manner, motive, process, and use of power still matter.

Relationships do not merely provide a setting in which leadership happens. They are one of the main places where God forms leaders.

## 5.4 Explore Scripture: A different kind of greatness

Read **Mark 10:35–45**.

James and John ask Jesus for positions of honour. The other disciples become angry, suggesting that competition for status affects the whole group. Jesus contrasts the rulers who dominate others with leadership among his followers. Greatness is expressed through service, following the pattern of the Son of Man.

Notice:

1. **Ambition can exist close to spiritual activity.** The disciples follow Jesus while still seeking status.
2. **Competition damages relationships.** The request affects the whole group.
3. **Jesus directly addresses the use of power.** Leadership among his people must have a different character.
4. **Service is not merely a technique.** It reflects the way of Jesus and his self-giving purpose.

Reflect:

- What were James and John seeking?
- Why might the other disciples have reacted so strongly?
- What forms can “lording it over” people take in ordinary leadership?
- How does Jesus redefine greatness without removing responsibility or influence?

## 5.5 Listening as an act of leadership

Listening does more than make people feel heard. It helps a leader understand reality, notice what they may have missed, and treat people as contributors rather than obstacles.

Listening does not require agreement. It requires enough patience and humility to understand before responding. A useful test is whether you can summarise the other person’s view in words they recognise as fair.

When relationships are difficult, leaders commonly:

- defend before understanding;
- avoid the person and speak to others instead;
- confuse disagreement with disloyalty;
- use spiritual or positional authority to end questions;
- soften expectations until no one knows what is required;
- focus on intention while ignoring impact.

A better conversation makes room for observation, perspective, effect, and next steps.

## 5.6 Four dimensions of formation

### 5.6.1 Head: Understand authority as stewardship

The central leadership principle is:

> **Leadership influence is entrusted for service, and listening helps us use that influence responsibly.**

Write briefly:

1. What authority or influence do you currently have?
2. Who is affected by the way you use it?
3. What is the difference between serving people and pleasing them?

### 5.6.2 Heart: Examine your relational reflex

When someone disagrees, disappoints, or criticises you, do you tend to:

- defend;
- withdraw;
- give in;
- control;
- blame;
- become overly agreeable;
- seek allies?

What fear or desire may drive that reflex? What would love, courage, and humility make possible?

### 5.6.3 Hands: Prepare a healthy conversation

Choose a real but manageable conversation.

| Step | Preparation |
|---|---|
| **Purpose:** What good outcome am I seeking? |  |
| **Observation:** What happened, without exaggeration? |  |
| **Perspective:** What do I need to understand from them? |  |
| **Effect:** How has the situation affected people or the work? |  |
| **Responsibility:** What part belongs to me? |  |
| **Next step:** What request, decision, or agreement is needed? |  |

Begin with curiosity. If safety, misconduct, harassment, abuse, or a serious power imbalance is involved, seek appropriate help rather than attempting an informal conversation alone.

### 5.6.4 Habits: Listen Before Responding

**Purpose:** To understand people more accurately and reduce defensive or controlling responses.

**Cue:** When someone raises a concern, disagrees, or gives feedback.

**Practice:**

1. Ask one open question: “Can you help me understand what concerns you?”
2. Listen without interrupting.
3. Summarise: “What I hear you saying is…”
4. Ask: “Have I understood you correctly?”
5. Only then respond, decide, or arrange a further conversation.

**Review:** After the conversation, note what you learned that you did not know at the beginning.

**Support:** Tell the group one setting in which you will practise.

# Facilitator guide

## 5.9 Purpose of the conversation

Participants should understand influence as entrusted for service, recognise one relational reflex, practise Listen Before Responding, and plan a healthy conversation.

## 5.10 Prepare as facilitator

- Read Mark 10:35–45 and the participant material.
- Prepare to model listening without quickly correcting an answer.
- Know the reporting and support pathways for serious relational concerns.

## 5.11 One-hour conversation plan

### 5.11.1 Reconnect and review the previous habit — 10 minutes

Pray briefly. Invite each person to describe how they practised Serve, Notice, Ask and one adjustment they want to make.

### 5.11.2 Enter through experience — 7 minutes

Ask:

> What does another person do that helps you feel genuinely heard?

Collect specific behaviours rather than general qualities.

### 5.11.3 Explore Scripture — 11 minutes

Discuss:

- What does the request reveal about the disciples’ view of leadership?
- How does competition for status affect the group?
- What misuse of power does Jesus reject?
- How can a leader serve while still making decisions and addressing problems?

### 5.11.4 Share and examine — 15 minutes

> **Halfway point:** The group will reach the halfway point of the one-hour conversation near the beginning of this activity.

Each participant names one relational reflex. Explore what triggers it, what it tries to achieve, how another person may experience it, and what alternative response is possible.

### 5.11.5 Practise Listen Before Responding — 8 minutes

In pairs, one person speaks for one minute about a manageable concern. The listener asks one open question, summarises, and checks understanding without giving advice. Switch roles and debrief:

- What helped you feel heard?
- What was difficult about not responding immediately?
- What did the listener learn?

### 5.11.6 Make a commitment — 6 minutes

Each person completes:

> When I speak with __________ about __________, I will first ask __________ and summarise what I hear.

### 5.11.7 Pray — 3 minutes

Pray for servant-hearted influence, courage in difficult conversations, and love expressed through careful listening.

## 5.12 Facilitator cautions

- Do not frame service as tolerating abusive or unsafe behaviour.
- Do not require reconciliation where safety, truth, or accountability has not been addressed.
- Do not let listening become a technique for appearing caring while retaining a fixed decision deceptively.
- Pay attention to power differences and organisational responsibilities.

## 5.13 Between sessions

Ask participants what they discovered by practising Listen Before Responding. Encourage them to record what they understood only after listening.

# Module 6: God Deepens Your Life With Him

> **Main idea:** God deepens your life with him so that your leadership increasingly flows from dependence, love, humility, and lasting spiritual fruit.
>
> **Main Scripture:** John 15:1–17
>
> **Leadership habit:** Abide and Review

## 6.1 Begin with life

A capable leader begins with prayer, dependence, and care for people. As responsibilities increase, time with God becomes shorter. The leader continues using familiar skills and produces results, so few people notice the change. Gradually, impatience grows, people become tasks, and activity replaces attentiveness.

The leader is still doing work for God but is finding less life with God in the work.

Skill, experience, and discipline matter. None can replace a living relationship with Jesus. Christian leadership becomes unsustainable when public activity is disconnected from inner life.

Leading people exposes the limits of skill and effort. Sustainable leadership requires a life that remains deeply connected to Jesus.

## 6.3 Beyond performance

Leaders can become dependent on visible results, past success, praise, control, or constant activity. These sources may sustain effort temporarily, but they cannot produce the kind of fruit Jesus desires.

Spiritual depth is not withdrawal from responsibility. It is learning to act from communion with God rather than from self-sufficiency. It includes:

- receiving identity before proving worth;
- listening before rushing into activity;
- allowing Scripture and prayer to shape desire and judgment;
- noticing when success is changing the heart;
- receiving pruning, correction, and limits;
- loving people rather than using them to accomplish goals;
- remaining teachable through every season;
- helping others grow rather than making oneself indispensable.

The practices that sustain one leader may differ from those that sustain another. The essential reality is dependence on Christ.

## 6.4 Explore Scripture: The vine and the branches

Read **John 15:1–17**.

Jesus speaks to his disciples on the night before his death. He uses the picture of a vine and branches. The branch does not manufacture life independently; it receives life by remaining connected. Jesus links remaining in him with his words, prayer, obedience, love, joy, and lasting fruit.

Notice:

1. **Fruit flows from connection.** The branch’s activity cannot replace its dependence.
2. **Pruning is part of fruitfulness.** Growth may include loss, correction, limits, and change.
3. **Remaining includes love and obedience.** It is more than a private feeling or occasional spiritual exercise.
4. **The fruit is relational.** Jesus commands his disciples to love one another.
5. **Fruit is meant to last.** Sustainable formation matters more than short-lived performance.

Reflect:

- What does the image of vine and branches reveal about dependence?
- How are remaining, obedience, love, prayer, joy, and fruit connected?
- What might a leader be tempted to produce apart from deep connection with Jesus?
- Where might pruning be different from punishment?

## 6.5 An integrated life

Throughout this course you have considered four dimensions:

- **Head:** truth and leadership understanding;
- **Heart:** character, motives, love, and integrity;
- **Hands:** service, relationships, skills, and gifts;
- **Habits:** repeated practices that support formation.

These dimensions belong together. Knowledge without love can become pride. Good intention without skill can cause preventable harm. Skill without character can misuse influence. A habit without a living purpose can become empty routine.

Remaining in Jesus gives the dimensions their centre. We learn truth to follow him, welcome inner formation to become like him, practise leadership to serve as he serves, and build habits that help us remain attentive and responsive.

Across this journey, God uses your story, forms your character, builds your faithfulness, develops your gifts, shapes how you lead people, and continues deepening your life with him.

### 6.6.4 Habits: Abide and Review

**Purpose:** To keep leadership activity connected with inner life, relationships, learning, and dependence on God.

**Cue:** Choose a consistent weekly time and place.

**Practice:** Spend 15–20 minutes with these movements:

1. **Abide:** Become still and attend to God’s presence.
2. **Give thanks:** Where did I notice grace, help, or fruit?
3. **Review:** What significant leadership moments occurred?
4. **Examine:** What happened within me and in my relationships?
5. **Listen:** What might Scripture, prayer, feedback, or circumstances be bringing to my attention?
6. **Respond:** What is one faithful next step?

**Review:** After four weeks, consider what themes are repeating and what support you need.

**Support:** Share one appropriate insight with a mentor, peer, or trusted leader each month.

This review is not a method for claiming certainty about everything God is doing. It is a practice of humble attention.

# Facilitator guide

## 6.10 Purpose of the conversation

Participants should connect sustainable leadership with remaining in Jesus, integrate their Head–Heart–Hands–Habits learning, name significant growth, and leave with a supported 30-day development plan.

## 6.11 Prepare as facilitator

- Read John 15:1–17 and the participant material.
- Review notes from earlier sessions and identify specific growth to affirm in each participant.
- Prepare your own brief example of a practice that helps keep leadership connected with God.
- Plan an appropriate way for the mentoring relationship to continue or conclude.

## 6.12 One-hour conversation plan

### 6.12.1 Reconnect and review the previous habit — 10 minutes

Pray briefly. Ask what participants learned by practising Listen Before Responding. What changed in the conversation or in them?

### 6.12.2 Enter through experience — 7 minutes

Ask:

> What signs tell you that leadership activity is becoming disconnected from your life with God?

Allow both inner and relational signs: hurry, irritation, numbness, control, neglect, loss of joy, or treating people as tasks.

### 6.12.3 Explore Scripture — 11 minutes

Discuss:

- What does the vine-and-branches picture teach about dependence?
- How do love and obedience keep “remaining” from becoming only a private feeling?
- What kinds of fruit matter in Christian leadership?
- How can we respond humbly to pruning, limits, or change?

### 6.12.4 Share and integrate — 15 minutes

> **Halfway point:** The group will reach the halfway point of the one-hour conversation near the beginning of this activity.

Each participant shares one significant insight and one continuing area of formation. Invite group members to offer one specific affirmation and one hope for that person’s growth.

Keep this grounded in observed patterns rather than grand predictions about the participant’s future.

### 6.12.5 Refine the 30-day plan — 8 minutes

Participants briefly present their plans. Help each person make the steps small, specific, connected to real life, and supported by someone. Confirm a review date.

### 6.12.6 Make a commitment — 6 minutes

Each person completes:

> For the next 30 days, my central formation focus is __________. I will hold my weekly review on __________, and I will ask __________ to support me.

If the group will continue meeting, agree on the next check-in. If not, identify another appropriate support relationship.

### 6.12.7 Pray and commission — 3 minutes

Pray for each participant by name, giving thanks for God’s grace and asking for enduring love, humility, courage, faithfulness, and fruit.

## 6.13 Facilitator cautions

- Do not imply that spiritual practices guarantee visible success or freedom from difficulty.
- Do not make the final session a performance review.
- Avoid exaggerated predictions or claims about God’s specific future plan for a participant.
- Do not leave development plans unsupported; help participants identify a real person and review date.

## 6.14 After the course

Arrange a 30-day follow-up if appropriate. Review the plan using four questions:

1. What did you practise?
2. What fruit or difficulty did you notice?
3. What are you learning about God, yourself, leadership, and people?
4. What will you continue, adjust, or stop?

Celebrate evidence of faithfulness and formation, not only visible accomplishment.
